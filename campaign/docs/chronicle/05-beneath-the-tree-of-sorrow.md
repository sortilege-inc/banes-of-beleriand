## Gifts

They stayed an extra day at Long Cleeve, and they spent it making ready.

The long-folk had been afraid of their weapons, and whatever the Company did, it could not go
back to the tree armed. So they would split. Már and Makheneb would stay out of sight with the
weapons, and the other three would go in empty-handed and try again to talk. Makheneb was not
the right person to carry a gift into that tree, he said, but he would help make one.

Marigold made the gift. The long-folk child had thrown down a pipe to them, and Már had let it
break; so Marigold carved a new one, a little recorder, and she did not try to make a masterpiece
of it. She made a pair of whistles as well, one for the three who went in and one for the two who
stayed out. If anything went wrong, whoever was in trouble would blow. They agreed on a place to
fall back to if they were separated, and that everyone would be back by nightfall. If they were
kept later than that, it might only be that they had been asked to dinner.

Marigold still had her drawing of the white beast under the floor. They talked about showing it to
the long-folk. Not yet, they decided. It would keep in her pocket until the long-folk did not hate
them.

Perry left his long axe and his bow with the others, and his buckler and helm with his horse. He
kept on the steel breastplate from the ruins. Lily left her spear and her short sword. They set out
on foot and found how much they had come to rely on horses; walking was slower, and if they meant
to be in and out by nightfall there was not much time to spare.

Makheneb found them a place to wait, close enough to the tree to hear a whistle.

## The recorder

Marigold went up to the tree playing the recorder. She tried to play the bear song on it. She had
made it only the day before, and like many children's toys it did not have all the notes, so what
came out was an approximation, with some of the fingering wrong. It did not sound bad. Perry and
Lily walked beside her in the open, as openly as they could, clapping along.

About a third of the way up the trunk there was a burl that served as a ledge. People began to come
out onto it, and out of the branches and the trunk, adults and children, eight or nine that they
could see on three or four limbs, all looking down with great curiosity. There were no weapons that
they could see. The song seemed to be going well.

After about five minutes a tall young woman came out from among the roots, where the young men had
come from before. Two or three of the young men followed her at fifteen paces, ready to act if
needed but otherwise only escorting her. One of them was the young man who had played tug-of-war
with Marigold over the bag. The woman spoke to them fluently, questioningly, and waited for an
answer.

Marigold did not know her language. She held out the recorder in both hands with her head bowed,
and then pointed to the children.

The young woman's brown hair was in tight ringlets bunched up on her head, and her skin, like all
the others', was dark and thick and leathery, like the skin of someone very old who has been out in
the sun all their life. She was plainly hardly more than a girl. She took the recorder, put it to
her lips, and looked at Marigold. When Marigold nodded, she tried a few notes. There was clapping
from above. She said something more, and beckoned, and led them in, playing a note or two as she
went. The young men sat down at the entrance as the three passed, and then rose and followed.

## Inside the tree

The inside of the tree was a township. The tree might have been many trees, or else part of its root
system had grown up above the ground, but either way the long-folk had built their whole town in it
and around it. A stair of roots curled up and around inside it, and the roots were shaped for use.
Perhaps the roots had moved to suit the people, or perhaps the people had bent them slowly as they
grew. It took the three of them up and out through a hole to the outside of the tree at the back,
already fifty feet above the ground, and on up.

It was dizzying. Young men and women, and grown folk too, went scampering along the branches like
squirrels, and leapt from one branch to another across gaps where a miss would drop them a couple
of hundred feet. Nobody was running from the visitors. This was only everyday life. An old one, in
their sixties perhaps, was climbing slowly up a ladder of vines.

The young woman brought them into a chamber like the hollow a bird or a squirrel makes in a tree,
only twenty feet across and thirty high. There were mats on the floor. It was a room for receiving
guests, or perhaps a throne room. Half a dozen people were gathered there, and in the middle an old
and wizened woman sat cross-legged on the ground. The young woman handed her the recorder. The old
woman beckoned the three of them forward, and began to ask them questions.

They had no words in common. Everything that followed was done by gesture and mime, by music, and
by slow and careful signs.

Lily began. She made a little curtsy, of the kind anyone who has worked at an inn knows how to
make, and pointed to each of them in turn: "Lily. Perry. Marigold." The young woman followed the
naming. She pointed to herself. "Fornbok." And back to Lily: "Lily." Then Lily told them, with a
great deal of gesture and a great deal of expression on her face, that their home was very
beautiful. Whatever else was not understood, that was.

## Safe

Marigold wanted to tell them why they had come. They were travelling, and searching, and what they
were searching for was a great thing, to make sure it was safe. She settled on the heart of it:
*safe.* That was what they were trying to do. They were looking to make things safe, to make them
good.

Fornbok reached for a switch that lay near her, a long thin whippy stick with a few leaves still on
it, and tapped Perry lightly on the chest where the breastplate was.

"Safe," she said.

It was the only word she spoke in their tongue.

Perry understood. If he wanted them to feel safe, he would have to show it. He took one for the
team. He unbuckled the steel breastplate and set it aside.

Fornbok nodded, and said it again, more gently. The room had changed towards them.

Lily took up the next part: *search.* She acted out searching as though she were playing it for a
room full of children, all hands and exaggeration, searching and searching, and then *safe*, and
then running into danger. Fornbok watched her thoughtfully. Then she gave a whistle so sharp that
their ears rang, and a child came and stood at her side. It was perhaps six or seven years old, and
it already stood past Marigold's shoulder. It waited for instruction.

Then Perry played the beast. He crouched and loomed and was enormous and terrible. Marigold and Lily
put him to sleep and covered him over, and they all had a quiet little celebration over his sleeping
body. It was the best acting Perry had ever done. He bowed at the end. Then he said, earnestly and
slowly, "Safe. We are not here to hurt anybody. We're only looking for information, to make sure
that you're all safe, and everybody else is too."

Two of the young men who had followed them up began talking excitedly to each other. Then they did a
mime of their own. One crouched low. The other came up behind him, and the first leapt and clutched
him about the shoulders and stabbed his fingers into the other's shoulder like teeth. The one who had
been bitten took his little gourd and poured it out on the ground, and lay down on the ground himself.

They knew something the Company did not.

The old woman nodded and said a few words, and pointed to the child, who came and took Marigold's
hand and began to lead her out. They were going to be shown round.

Marigold showed them the map first, and pointed from it to the drawing, trying to ask where. It meant
nothing to them. Lily thought they might never have seen a map before.

## The pit

The child led them down the way they had come and further down still, through a labyrinth of passages
among the roots, down past the entrance where they had come in and on under the tree. It was very dark
down there. Here and there something grew on the roots that gave off a faint light, and the passages
glowed dimly. It was two or three in the afternoon, and there were a few hours left.

The two young men who had done the mime came with them. One of them was carrying Perry's breastplate.
Where the passage split, the child pulled them to the right, and where it split again, to the right
again. Then the young man with the breastplate stepped forward and threw it.

There was a pit there, and it was full of weapons and armour and metal things, old and broken, a great
heap of them. The breastplate landed on top.

Perry lunged to catch it, missed, and went into the pit after it. He landed among a great deal of metal,
none of it comfortable. He was not badly hurt and he did not get skewered. He put the breastplate on so
as to have both hands free to climb out. As he came up, one of the young men put a foot to his shoulder
and pushed him gently back.

"Sorry," said Perry. "I know that wasn't graceful." He tried again, and was pushed back again, and at
last he understood. He stood in the pit for a while. "So what are we here for? When can I leave?"
There was no food down there, but he had his Ranger rations. He could last a few days.

In the end he took the breastplate off and left it. He had only had it three days, though he had given
up his old corslet for it, and it weighed as much as half of everything else he carried together. He
tried to throw it back up out of the pit, and could not; it was too heavy.

There was so much metal down there, and so much of it old and ill-used, that there might have been
something valuable in it if anyone had the time to dig. Anything of leather had rotted. Marigold looked
at it all and at the hand of the child who had led her, which was tough in the way their skin was tough,
though not calloused. Everything in the pit was metal, weapons or armour or smith's work of some kind.
The long-folk's own weapons were simple things, sticks and staves made from what grew here, with nothing
of the forge about them. This was a whole class of things that was forbidden to them. It was not being
kept here. It was thrown away. The long-folk had been confiscating such things for generations and
dumping them here without ceremony.

Perry climbed out without the breastplate, and they went on.

## The well

The passage ended in a small chamber with a well in it. Great jars of clay stood about, like the old wine
jars of the south. The well was only a ring of roots about half a foot high, with the water just below
the rim, so that you could dip from it. The young men drew a ladle from one of the jars, which had a lid
that came off easily, took a sip themselves, and offered it round. They did not press.

Perry had just climbed in and out of a pit and was thirsty. He looked into the well first, in case there
was anything under the water. He had seen a great beast under a floor, and he was suspicious of the ground
now. There was nothing. He drank, and the others drank after him, Marigold only after smelling it.

It was very bitter, with a sharp earthy taste, something like pine sap. Once it was down, their hearts
began to race, as though they had drunk something to keep them awake for a week.

## The grotto

Back at the place where the passages first forked, the young men sent the child away. Then they pointed
down the other passage, where a small cold draught came up to meet them, and said something. They would
not go down it, and they had not wanted to talk about it where the child could hear. The tour was over.
They were ready to go back up as soon as the Company was.

The Company went down the other passage. One of the young men caught Perry by the arm, not firmly
enough to hold him, only to ask him not to.

"Safe," said Perry, and patted his hand, and went on.

He walked into the dark as if he owned it, and it heartened the others. It was like going through a
frightening part of a strange town with a local who strolls along saying *this way; it's fine*.

It was not fine. The place pressed on Lily and on Perry. It was unnatural to live in a tree to begin
with, and to live under the ground, and down here there was both. Marigold felt none of it. There are
plenty of hobbit-holes that are nothing but tunnels into a hill. This one wanted airing and lighting and
furnishing, but it did not feel wicked to her.

The glow of the roots was fading. Perry lit his lantern. The path sloped down, and the roots arched
overhead and reached forward along it like fingers. Then there was a sudden lip, a drop of a few feet,
and several small steps down, and at the bottom of them the water came right to the last step. It was a
pool, forty or fifty feet across, and the roots of the tree had made a grotto about it.

Lily looked about from where she stood. Across the pool and a little to the right, the roots bulged out
and made a pocket, and in it she could just see a crevice, and a nest or a space of some kind.

Marigold went round the edge of the pool to see. It was strange, but not terrifying, and there were no
giant white beasts under the water. She came to the place Lily had seen, where the roots bent outward.
In it there was a woman.

Marigold saw her face in profile. She was standing, leaning a little backward, with her feet planted and
her eyes closed. She might have been dead. She was bald. She was not as tall as the long-folk and did not
have their look; the nearest thing Marigold could think of was an Elf, but she did not truly look like
anything Marigold had ever seen. Things grew out of the roots, or perhaps they were the roots, and they
cradled her body. There was a wrapping about her, and Marigold could not see her arms and legs.

She came back round the pool, carefully, and told the others. She thought the woman was asleep. None of
them had seen anything else down there.

## Már and Makheneb

Outside, Már and Makheneb had not been idle.

As soon as the others had gone, Már bundled all the weapons together at the foot of a tree and covered
them with leaves. The two of them could have carried everything between them, but not easily, and not
if they had to move quickly. Then they hid. Makheneb found a place where he could see the pile. Már
stayed on the ground. That was the long-folk's country up in the trees; they were squirrel people.

The long-folk came, four of them, stealing through the forest to take the two unawares, and not doing it
well. Makheneb and Már both saw them coming, looked at each other, and kept still. One of them passed so
close to Makheneb that he was two feet away, looking straight past him. Then one of them noticed the
heap of leaves and began to brush them off, and found the weapons.

They were armed with staves, sharpened sticks. They were not threatening anyone. One picked up Perry's
buckler and looked at it and tucked it under his arm, and reached for more.

"That's our stuff," said Már. And she went.

She is a Dwarf, and Dwarves can sprint. She came out of her hiding place running hard and bellowing,
*No, no, no!*, and pulled up short about an arm's length from them. They could not understand a word
of it, but they understood very well that something was happening that she cared about a great deal.
At the same moment an arrow thudded into the ground a foot in front of the one holding the buckler.
Nobody saw where it had come from. He dropped the buckler.

For a moment no one moved.

"Mine," said Már, and pointed to the weapons, and to herself. She did not reach for her axe. *This is
ours. It isn't yours. We don't want to fight.* It was the kind of lesson you teach to small children.

They put back what they had picked up. They stepped away, and then they stood, two on one side of the
pile and two on the other, facing her. They did not threaten her. But they were standing guard over it,
and one of them, looking about, spotted Makheneb, pointed straight at him, and called out, and pointed
at the ground.

Makheneb stayed where he was, a hundred or two hundred feet behind Már, with his bow ready. Már stepped
towards the pile. The two nearest stepped between her and it. They did not touch it, but they would not
let her touch it either. One of them began to take a step forward, and Makheneb put an arrow into the
ground exactly where his foot was about to land. The foot went back.

So they waited, in a standoff.

## Goodbyes

Up in the tree the three said their goodbyes to Fornbok and the old woman and all the rest, and everyone
looked at them with a mixture of awe and concern. With a great deal of patience, and gestures they had
learned that day, the long-folk tried to tell them something, and they understood more of it than they
would have thought possible. It had been a couple of generations at least, the long-folk said, since *she*
had last woken. And when she woke they had put her back to sleep, at great cost to themselves.

Lily told them, as best she could, that she was sorry that had happened, and glad the woman was asleep.
Then they left.

They came out confidently, waving and happy, and found Már standing over the pile of weapons with four
long-folk on guard and Makheneb out of sight behind her with an arrow on the string.

Már did not call out to them; she did not want the long-folk to think she was signalling an attack. She
made the gesture for *weapons down*, and tried to persuade the guards. It did not work.

Perry, who had come bursting into the clearing in the middle of telling the story of their afternoon,
about a lady named Fornbok and all the bald people and how great they all were, stopped dead. "I can't
lose any more of my stuff," he said.

So Lily sang. She sang the bear song, loudly, and as she sang she used the gestures and the signs she had
seen the tree-folk use over and over while they talked to her, and Marigold joined in behind her. The guards
did not understand all of it, but they understood that these strangers had come from the tree, and that
they were speaking in ways the tree-folk spoke. They backed off. They went and stood between the Company
and the Tree of Sorrow, and gave them room.

The Company picked up their gear and went the other way.

## The vampire, perhaps

When they were well away, they tried to make sense of it all.

Had they found the bane? A lady, asleep in a nest inside a tree. They had not seen any fangs. But the young
men's mime had been of something that leapt on your back and bit. Nobody knew whether the long-folk had a
word for *vampire*.

Was she frozen, like the man in the ruins? No, said Marigold. Only asleep. There had been no glass wall in
front of her, nothing like the floor that held the white beast. At the first place they had been checking
locks. Here there was no lock. There were only the long-folk, who kept her asleep, at great cost, and who
said she woke every couple of generations. That did not seem to be now. They would make a note of it. Tom
should know.

Was she contained? They did not really know whether the white beast was, either. The first place had had
more than one defence: the doors, the wheels, the floor. Here there were the tree-folk. What more could
they do? Not wake her, certainly.

They noticed one more thing. In the pit, the things that had kept their shape were the metal ones. Anything
of wood or leather had rotted.

Most of all they needed someone who knew more. Lily still thought the Grey Havens were some kind of home for
Elves who had fallen on hard times, and so, she said, they should go there, and perhaps somebody there could
talk to the tree-folk. They would at least look like Elves.

It was a long walk back. The moon was high when they came to Long Cleeve, a few hours later than they had
meant to, though for the last hours they were on hobbit roads in country they knew, and not in the middle
of a forest with who knew what in it. They slept in the stable again.

## Frogmorton

In the morning they decided to go to the Grey Havens, and from there, perhaps, up to the Blue Mountains.
Nobody was sure they could simply turn up at the Grey Havens. It was not only for Elves, Makheneb thought,
but he did not know the customs for those who were not Elves; he was not even entirely sure of them for
himself. He had met fewer than five Elves of Lindon in his life. Those who went to the Grey Havens did not
come back to tell about it.

Before they went west, they wanted to see Tom. They had called on him once from Barad Tarminalë and he had
been slow to come. This time they would go towards him. They called, and they set off across the Shire
towards the Old Forest.

It was a notable company to be passing along the roads of the Shire, and hobbits sitting on their porches
or by the fruit stalls were forever flagging them down to hear their stories. The Company was polite, but
it had no wish to spend half a day at every stop explaining itself, and by now it had taken on a certain
frostiness, which was the only way to get past.

In Frogmorton a voice called out, "Mum!" It was Marigold's daughter, Flora.

They embraced, and Flora was delighted to see her and wanted to hear all about her adventures. Then she put
her hand on her mother's. "I have the best news," she said. "You're going to be a grandmother." It would be
five or six months yet, she thought.

"Then you'll have to come with me," said Marigold. "I can't bear to be away from my grandchild."

Lily was thrilled, and joined in, and promised shoulder rubs all the way. Marigold told Flora everything:
the wolves and the bald lady and the mushrooms and the bear song and the tree, all tumbling out at once. Flora
listened with a kind of stunned look, trying to take it all in. Her husband Robin was listening too. Marigold
had always been well disposed to Robin. By two-thirds of the way through the story he was shuddering, and
by the end, when it was clear she meant to take Flora with her into the Old Forest, he had heard enough.

With Robin's encouragement, Flora told her mother that they would gladly be there for her when she came back
from the Old Forest, and that she must come back to Frogmorton again soon, and stay with them for dinner, and
come back and stay when the baby was near. Stay *with them*, they meant. But they would not be travelling.

Marigold was heartbroken. The others said, in her support, that everything she had told them was true, and
Robin shuddered again. Then the Company sang them *Ghostcleavers*. Perhaps the words would make them think
better of the whole business. They sang well. Flora and Robin said that it was certainly not for them, but
they could appreciate the feats of Már the Dwarf, which was more a Dwarf's sort of thing anyway, and they were
glad Marigold had someone as strong and capable as Már with her. And Lily and Perry seemed to have their heads
on the right way round. She would probably be all right.

## Tom on the bridge

When they came to the Brandywine Bridge, Tom was sitting on the edge of it, kicking his feet and smoking his
pipe.

"I thought you'd all be coming this way!"

"Tom, we've got news," said Marigold. "One really important thing and one more mildly important. The really
important thing: my daughter's pregnant. She's going to have a baby!"

"That's delightful!"

The mildly important thing was the Tree of Sorrow, north of Hobbiton. They told him about the long-folk.
The matriarch's name, Perry said, as near as he could make it out, was Lingdadh.

"They've been here since before the Elves, I think," said Tom. "They're still here? They used to be all over
the place. I don't know where most of them went. They seem to have misplaced themselves."

There had been two near confrontations with them, they told him, but no blood drawn, and in the end they had
been invited in. And in the roots, where they had suspected a bane might be, they had found a sleeping woman
who looked something like an Elf. They had not seen any fangs. She was bald. Was she perhaps an old
acquaintance of his?

Tom looked at them with concern. "Well," he said. "Yes, this does seem concerning. It's not familiar to me.
But it sounds as if the tree-folk have it in hand. The Elves may know more of it, especially the Elves of the
West."

They were going to the Grey Havens, they told him. They had heard there was some sort of help given to Elves
there, and perhaps there would be old Elves they could talk to.

"That's wonderful," said Tom. "Would you take them something for me?" He patted his shirt, and wondered where
he had put it. He was wearing several layers, and at last he reached into an inner pocket and brought out a
honeycomb, all goopy, and handed it over. "Give this to Círdan, with my best wishes."

"Who is Círdan?"

"He builds boats, over in the Grey Havens." Tom spelled it out for them. "I owed him this a while back. If you
wish to have a nibble, you're welcome, so long as some of it gets to him." Perry had a nibble. Then he put the
honeycomb in a pocket.

They were thinking of the Dwarves of the Blue Mountains too. Did Tom have anything he wanted taken there?

"They haven't asked anything of me in many a century," said Tom. "They like to be self-sufficient." But when
they asked for advice, he said, quite nonchalantly, "Fenja, who is Lif, is trustworthy, and knows many deep
things."

Már was aghast. Tom had just given away a Dwarf's secret name, the name no one but a Dwarf's nearest know, as
casually as the weather. "We shouldn't be talking about her other name here," she said. "That's her name. It's
for her to know."

Tom did not understand the problem, but he understood that Már was upset, and he apologised. "Fenja I consider
trustworthy," he said. "I didn't mean to offend." Már did not know Fenja. But she knew her secret name now,
and she was not pleased about it.

Marigold asked whether Goldberry might have any more of whatever she had given them before; it would be
wonderful for the baby. Goldberry was otherwise engaged, Tom said, but he would get some jam to them before they
left the Shire. And Marigold made him promise to come and see the baby when it came. "A newborn hobbit," said
Tom. "It's been a while."

He offered to help them slip past all the prying relatives between here and the west of the Shire. They thanked
him and said no. It was a comfort, after the loneliness of the wild, to be among kin.

Tom looked closely at Perry. "The twins are doing well in Bree," he said.

Perry was a good uncle. He was very close to his brother's twins, and spent a good deal of his wages on presents
for them and outings, and tried to make them excited about keeping watch on the wall with him, which was not
exciting at all. It had been hard to be away from his family for so long, and harder still to be among other
people's families.

"That's good," said Perry. "Thank you. Thank you."

Tom saw that Strawberry was doing well, and the loan of her was renewed.

## Michel Delving

They went west across the Shire to Michel Delving, the nearest thing the hobbits have to a capital, where the
Mayor keeps a fine house and where there is a museum called the Mathom-house. Marigold knew it by reputation. It
was famous for its cookbooks and its family trees, its collections of hobbit-lore, and its old weapons and maps.

Már needed a pony. She did not want to go on sharing a horse. She had some of her treasure from the ruins left
to trade, a ring, the hairband, and one goblet, though she meant to keep the badger necklace. At the inn, where
they took rooms, she asked the innkeeper whether he could point her to anyone who might sell. She wanted
something strong, with stamina, that could carry her and her gear a long way for a long time. She kept it
low-key about what she could pay.

Lily had worked at an inn all her life, and she could see at once that it was not going the way Már wanted. The
innkeeper was humouring her, doing the least he could to tick the box. Lily stepped in beside Már and put in a
word or two, and got a little further. Most people had their ponies and liked them, the innkeeper said, and
though some were for sale, the sellers did not much like the sound of ponies leaving town for who knew where.
But he gave them a couple of names.

The pony they found was not the glorious beast Már had pictured, but it was not old or sickly either. It was a
young chestnut with a lighter mane, lively but not wild, and its name was Charlie. Már kept the name.

At the Mathom-house they looked for maps. The best of them covered a great sweep of country south of the Shire,
from the South Downs down along the Greyflood almost to Lond Daer where it met the sea, with Swanfleet and
Tharbad marked. The towns on it were few; it was not about towns. It was about flowers. There were several
versions of it, and they realised it was a series made over time. Someone had tracked six rare kinds of flower
across a hundred years, how common they were, and how their ground had changed.

The curator apologised to them. Some young hooligans had lately made off with several items from the
Mathom-house, and they were looking for the culprits. People were usually much better behaved.

The Company thanked her. They would be going down that way at some point, they said, and the map had been very
helpful.
