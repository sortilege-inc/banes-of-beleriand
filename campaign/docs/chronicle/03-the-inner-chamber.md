## What the ghost had been

The great doors stood open a crack, and nobody went through them at once.

"I've seen a few ghosts in my day," said Már. "That one was the most sinister I have ever met."

"You see why Findemir was bringing you along," somebody said. "You and ghosts, split with an axe."

Perry knew the ghost. He had known the man it had been. "We tried to bar him from Bree," he said, "and he
still got in. He went round by a back way."

What had the man been doing here? He had tried to take a piece of the wheel away, and with a piece
missing the doors would never open. Was that what he had wanted, to keep them shut? Or had somebody
sent him, as somebody had sent them, only to do the opposite? He had not seemed the leading kind to
Perry. He had seemed like the kind of man someone sends to do their dirty work. And if someone had
sent him, there might be others.

They had tramped all over the floor by now, but they looked for footprints anyway, working round the
room from the door, past the wheel, and on to the place where the body lay. They could tell their own
prints from any others: this was Makheneb's, and this was Lily's. Once those were set aside, the dead
man's movements could be followed through the dust. Following them, they found a ledge in a dark
corner of the wall, a little above head height and nowhere near the doors. On it was a Ranger's
pack. Inside was dried food of the kind that keeps in a cool place underground: an emergency store.
It was not food for a hard march. It was enough to keep one person from starving for two or three
weeks, until they could get somewhere better.

"A gift from Findemir," said somebody. If they were shut in behind those doors, there was food for
one of them for three weeks, or for all five of them for about five days.

Near the doors they found a second set of prints, not the dead man's. They went round and round the
wheel of stone, and then they went in through the doors, into the inner chamber. They did not come
out again. The doors had been opened after all, and not so long ago.

Már looked at the skeleton. Undead were her trade, and she knew how odd deaths looked. Round its neck
was a necklace of Dwarvish make, with a badger worked on it in profile. She did not believe for a
moment that the man had come by it honestly. She took it, and wearing it she felt a little more
formidable than before.

Then she read the bones as Perry had read the floor. The man had fallen several feet away from the
wall, and then he had pushed himself backward along the ground until the wall stopped him. He had
been trying to get away from something. She thought he had died of terror.

"Did he just need his friend to open the door," Lily wondered, "and then his friend said goodbye?"

## The stair

The doors were higher than the chamber's own ceiling and half again as wide as a room. They had
opened inward just a crack, a couple of feet across. There were handles; to swing a door wide would
have taken two of them hauling together, and it might have been loud. They slipped through the gap
instead.

Beyond, a wide staircase went down, as wide as the doors. Here were the ruins proper. But nothing had
come in here in earnest, and it had been sealed for most of its long life, so it was not ruined at
all, only old and unattended. The stonework was beautiful. The floor was level and the stairs were
true stairs. Lily badly wanted to touch the stone.

Should they prop the door? They had nothing to do it with, short of the skeleton, and no one wanted to
touch that. In the end they simply went on.

Going quietly was impossible. They had no light but the lanterns, and the lanterns would draw
anything that was waiting for them; and stairs are loud. They went down together, the stairs wide
enough for all of them, with one of them three steps behind to watch the way they had come. The stair
curved gently to the left, not a corkscrew but a slow turn, and went down three or four storeys.

## The chamber

At the bottom there were lamps along the walls, ready to be lit. The floor gave off a faint chill.
It looked almost crystalline, a pale blue, like frosted glass. It crunched a little underfoot, like
ice, though it did not feel like true ice. The light of their lanterns died away before it reached
the ceiling. On the walls were beautiful geometric patterns in tile, of the kind usually set into a
floor; a few had fallen, but most were whole.

"They'll see us regardless," somebody said. "We might as well be able to see them." Perry began
lighting the lamps as he passed.

As the lamps caught one after another, the room came out of the dark. It was very large, some two
hundred and fifty feet across, and nearly eight-sided. The ceiling arched up to a small, shallow dome.
There were three side rooms. In the middle was a wheel of stone like the one outside: a wide, flat
round of stone, but four times as broad. And standing on it was a man.

He was holding the hilt of a sword. The rest of the blade lay in pieces about his feet. He was bent
over the stone as though he had been trying to prise it open, or break it, and he was absolutely
still.

He had skin. He was not a skeleton, and he was not quite a statue. He looked like a perfect likeness
of himself, in all his colours, only drained of them and turned dull and drab. There was no
breath and no movement in him. Marigold touched him. His skin was cold, like cold stone, and yet
there was still some give to it. He had not been turned to stone. He had simply stopped.

The sight of him chilled them all.

The sword had been a long sword. "That's a noble's weapon," somebody said. "It's not what the other
one would carry." Perhaps this was the man who had hired him.

Perry looked at the hilt. It bore a bird of prey, an eagle perhaps. He had seen something like it once
before, though not the same. A Ranger who came through Bree had carried a sword with a hilt of that
kind, and had said it was an heirloom of his forefathers, and that the first pieces of it went back
to the Second Age, thousands of years ago. This was not that sword, but it came from the same family
of things. It was as old as this place.

The man had been trying to drive the blade into the stone like a wedge. There was hardly a seam to
drive it into, only a tiny crack.

The Bree-folk had seen enough people come through their town to judge him. He was a Man, and they
guessed he came from further south than Tharbad. He was no Ranger.

## The side rooms

Makheneb went round the three side rooms while the others stood by the wheel.

The first was a small living space, with room for two or three to sleep, a chest, some blankets left
behind, a table, and a whetstone. Anyone who needed to stay here for a while could.

The second was for reading and writing. It had a rough wooden chair and a table, slanted shelves for
scrolls, and a shelf and a half of books bound in leather. The scroll-shelves were two-thirds full.
There was a jar of ink. It was a spartan room; the books and scrolls were by far the most valuable
things in it.

The third was a store. Spare furniture for the other rooms was kept there, and shelves of things
wrapped in cloths and leather. Makheneb unwrapped some of them. They were relics, extremely old, some
of precious metal and some of exquisite craft. At the back were a few weapons and some armour, of
strange style and interesting workmanship.

Before he said a word to anyone, he went over it for any taint. He was more learned in the Shadow than
the others. None of it felt like the two dead men. The things in this room looked as though they had
always been here, and were meant to be. There was also a bow there, a very fine one. He took it.

Then he came back and told the others that there was treasure in there, some kind of armoury, and he
was not sure it was theirs to take. Perhaps it was the sort of place where you take a bowl and leave a
bowl.

## The wheel, and what was under the floor

Marigold compared the carving on the great wheel with the journal. It matched perfectly. She did not
know the language, but she had heard of protective charms, and of wards, and of words that seal
something shut. She thought this might be one. If so, what mattered was that the words were whole.
They were whole. They were here to check on things, and this checked out.

Lily was all for leaving the frozen man. Moriel had told them to check the symbols, not to rescue
people who had been caught trying to break them. But nobody liked the thought of leaving somebody who
might still be alive.

While they talked, Marigold saw something. A faint vapour, almost too slight to see, was rising from
the crack in the stone where the man had been prying. She felt a slight chill from it before it
thinned away. "We should get him off that," she said.

Then Perry and Lily looked down.

The floor under their feet was beautiful, and they had thought it opaque. Something shifted, and they
saw that it was not as opaque as they had thought. The whole floor was a window. Under it was a beast.
Its back had been pressed up against the underside of the glass, and now it rolled over.

They saw it in profile. It was a couple of hundred feet long. It had fur like a bear's, white, and
huge horns and tusks. It was shaped more like a man than a dragon; if they had known of such a thing,
they would have thought of an ape of monstrous size. It was asleep. It had only rolled over in its
sleep. None of them had heard, even in a fairy-story, of anything like it.

The dread of it went through all of them. Then it settled, and it did not move again.

## The ledger

Nobody wanted to stay long after that. But the journal said that the one who came to check should add
an entry to the log, and Marigold meant to do it properly.

They tiptoed into the reading room. The book lying readiest to hand was the log. Every few decades
there was a new entry, and most of them were perfunctory: the date, who had come, that they had checked
the things they were meant to check and stayed three days, and that all was well. Sometimes they added
something else they had done or noticed in the country round about. Year after year after year, it
went back. Even the recent entries were written in a kind of shorthand that Marigold guessed belonged
to the Rangers. Going further back, the writing grew harder and harder to read, until a couple of
centuries back she could not read it at all.

There was nothing in it about a monster. There was nothing about a frozen man.

It was, Marigold thought, like the note on the wall of a privy that says who cleaned it last and
whether the soap had been filled. And now here was she: *Marigold was here*, not looking at the
monster the whole time.

She wrote the date and a full account of what they had found. In the margins she described the sleeping
beast. Then, with a good deal of skill, she drew it. The head and the face came out especially well,
and she caught the shape of the rest. It took her the best part of an hour.

Lily read what she could meanwhile. The scrolls were in a different, older language, in a much more
flowing hand. Even in the books she could not read, the years were numbered in a way she could follow,
and she could see that some of the earliest were five hundred or seven hundred years old. The entries
varied, too. In one book an entry ran to eight or ten pages, and the next ran to a page and a half, and
the next to half a page. That long one was from about a hundred and fifty years ago. Someone, then, had
met with something. Lily could not read what.

After three-quarters of an hour Lily was frightened and fascinated at once, and after an hour she was
making frantic gestures at the others to wrap it up. Was the thing asleep for the winter, or only
taking a nap? What had done this to the man? She did not like that he looked petrified and not dead,
with that underneath him, and she wanted to be a long way from the floor.

## The armoury

Perry went to the storeroom to see whether there was anything in it that might help them if the beast
got loose. There were some daggers. There was a gap where a sword had been, and Perry guessed it was
the sword the frozen man had broken. There was another gap where a bow had been — the one Makheneb had
taken. There were a great many arrows and arrowheads, a halberd, and some breastplates, ornamental ones
by the look of them, with crests none of them knew.

A breastplate sounded good to Perry. Steel had to be better than leather. He put one on in place of his
corslet. It was made for a man a good foot taller than he was, but not so much larger that he could not
wear it.

The arrowheads looked new and fit for use, not decoration. Makheneb did not trust the old shafts, but
the heads were good, and he could fletch new arrows easily enough. Arrowheads were not really treasure,
he reasoned. He took some, and dropped a few more where the others could pick them up.

Már went to the store as well, to see whether there was any sign of the dead in it. There was none;
but there were the shelves. There were candelabra, and goblets, and pottery that had kept its shape
though its colours had faded, all of it exquisite, and some of the craftsmanship was new even to a
Dwarf. She meant to take only a little, and she took more. She took two goblets, a ring worked with a
bird, and a band of gold for her hair set with very tasteful gems. It would look good on her.

Makheneb gathered up the shards of the broken sword and wrapped them in a cloth, and worked the hilt out
of the frozen man's hands. The hands were firm enough that the hilt came away on its own. It might be
reforged somewhere.

## The frozen man

In the end they would not leave him. It would be wrong to leave a man who might be alive to die down
there. And if he knew something, it might be worth a great deal.

They tied him to a cot from the sleeping room, for they did not trust him not to wake up possessed, and
a couple of them carried him. His limbs could be moved, they found, but they did not dare try. Perry put
out every lamp he had lit. They carried the man up the stairs and out into the outer chamber, and pushed
the great doors shut behind them. There was no click, but the doors felt secure.

Far away up the passage, the way they had come, something whined, high and thin. Then something howled.

Marigold felt a cold place in the room. Past the stone wheel, near the skeleton, there was a lump on the
floor that had not been there when they went in. It looked like a lump of ice, and it was changing. As
she watched, a paw came out of it, and then another, not quite finished. It was making itself out of
the ice.

"We have to go," she said. "We have to go."

"Light me an arrow," said Makheneb.

They went on up with the frozen man between them, and Makheneb walked backwards with his bow, waiting.
The thing's face began to form. It was the face of one of the wolves they had fought. He shot it through
the head with a burning arrow just as it found its shape. The fire went on burning inside it, and it
burned from the inside out. It had not even formed well enough to howl. It gave a pale whimper, and fell
in on itself, and melted away.

At the top of the long slope the mouth of the cave showed against the evening light. There was a wolf
there, one only, looking in and down. In the pit, something was still moaning.

They charged it all together, and Már led them. She was carrying the frozen man's feet, and she let
them go and bellowed and went forward, staring at the wolf with the badger at her throat. She was the
bigger dog. The wolf froze where it stood and met her eyes. Then it yelped, and turned, and ran.

They went round the pit. The wolf that had fallen in was dead now on the stakes. As they came out of the
cave they could see, far off on the other side, three wolves running away over the horizon.

## By night

They went up to the ruins for the horses. They had four mounts for the five of them and the frozen
man, and they doubled up; the frozen man rode on Leaper in front of Perry. They would not reach Barad
Tarminalë before dark. It would be three or four hours after nightfall. They decided to push on, since
there would be a good rest at the end of it.

On the way they stopped for an hour to see whether the man could be woken. Somebody felt for a pulse.
There was none, and no breath. Nothing that had happened, not even all the jolting, had stirred him.
Lily brought out her healing balm.

Makheneb waited for nightfall. Then he laid the man down on a bed of leaves and tried the ways of his
own people, the Elvish ways, and set himself to drive out whatever darkness had come into him. The
healing did nothing. But as he worked, a realisation came over him. He had dealt with things born of
the evil of Dol Guldur, and things near to it. This came from an evil as great as that, or greater, and
there was nothing in him that could undo it. The man was not dead. But Makheneb knew of no way to bring
him back.

"This is some bad magic," he told the others.

They searched the man's pockets. There were no marks on him. Inside one pocket they found a slip of paper
rolled up and tied to the lining, so that it could not be lost. When they unrolled it there was a
passphrase written on it, like the call or the answer that gets you in at a locked door.

## Ghostcleavers

They came to Barad Tarminalë in the dark, and rested there.

They called for Tom, as Tom had told them they might in need, but Tom did not answer.

In the days of rest that followed, they made a song of victory for Már. They called it *Ghostcleavers*.

About five days later, Marigold and Lily woke early, as they always did, and found Tom Bombadil in the
kitchen cooking breakfast.

Tom looked at the frozen man. He said plainly that he could not undo what had been done to him.

Then Marigold showed him her drawing of the white beast under the floor, and Tom changed. He knew it.
There were several of these beings, he told them, bound or sleeping in secret places. He took Perry's
map, and on it he marked where others lay: in the North Moors, near the Shire; south of Tharbad; and in
the Blue Mountains.

## The road west

When the Company set out west, they fell in with another company on the road, a curious and peaceful
one. There was Celenneth, a Ranger of the Dúnedain, and Linnea, a woman of Bree, who was her partner;
their children, Brynja, the older girl, and Hallas, who was still small; and with them Eryndil, a
scholarly Elf of Lindon.

The two companies travelled together for a time under the early summer sky, and told each other their
stories, and what they meant to do.
