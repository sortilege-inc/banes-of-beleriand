## Már

Már had been sent north without being told why.

She was a Dwarf of Durin's folk, a hundred and seventeen years old, and among her clan she was the
one they sent for when something dead would not stay dead. In the mountains there are always things
that creep into the places where people live, and she had a knack for finding them and putting an
end to them. That was her work, and she was known for it. So when a Ranger of the North came to her
clan on some errand of his own, it was Már they told to go with him. Nobody explained what the
errand was. The Ranger, Findemir, plainly knew where he was going and what he meant to do there, and
he talked to her as though she knew it too. She did not. She went anyway.

Findemir would not ride. He insisted on going on foot, and never said why, and so they left their
mounts at the Rangers' post and walked north together into the hills.

The land there was low and rolling, with no forest to speak of, only stands of trees here and there.
It had been settled once and had long since gone wild. Further south the spring was in full flood,
with weeds and flowers bursting into bloom along every road; but these hills seemed not to have
heard of it. The buds were late. Snow still lay in the lee of the slopes and in the valleys where
the shade kept it.

The wolves came on them a few hours out. There were four, and they were not like any wolves Már had
seen. They were dire wolves, white-furred and huge, and they were cold. The air about them frosted
as they moved, though it was spring and the day was mild. Each one was like a walking blizzard, and
the biggest was bigger than the rest by a good deal.

Findemir and Már set their backs to one another and stood on their guard. Two wolves went for each
of them. Már watched for the openings and made the wolves' strikes go wide, and she beat them off,
but the fight went against them. It is hard to judge the reach of a wolf when there are two of them
and they take turns. The great wolf worked its way in on Findemir. Something drew his eye for an
instant, and in that instant it was at his throat.

He went down. Már saw the blood and saw the wolves close in on him. She could have run. Instead she
went in over his body to drive them off him, and one of them snapped at her as she came and another
caught her by the leg.

## Four out of the south

The Company had left Barad Tarminalë that morning on Moriel's directions, following Findemir. They
heard the snarling from over a rise, and came over the top of it to find a Dwarf alone among four
wolves with a man down at her feet.

Makheneb had an arrow on the string before the others had taken it in. He picked the wolf that had
just bitten the Dwarf, the next most dangerous after the great one, and loosed. It was a long shot,
and the arrow from his great two-handed bow went in with a thud they heard from the rise. The wolf
squealed. The arrow stood sideways out of its ribs.

Perry had a bow, though he had not drawn one in earnest in a long while; his arrow went well wide.
Marigold had not shot anything in twenty years, and hers fell almost at her feet. Then they were
down the slope and in among the wolves.

Perry came in hard and swung his long-hafted axe at one of them. He did not cleave it. He clipped it,
and the weight of the blade knocked it sideways and left it winded and staggering.

Lily had grown up on a farm, and all her training with a spear had been mock fights with her cousins
in the yard. She told herself this was the same thing. It was the biggest and most savage animal she
had ever seen, and she was in a panic, and she put her spear through it with both hands and killed it
outright. When she pulled the spear free the head of it was white with frost. It was so cold that if
she had touched her tongue to it, it would have stuck.

Marigold went for the great wolf with her axe. Her nerve failed her at the last. At home she had had
a dog called Bobo, one of those big Shire dogs that stand as tall as a hobbit and are as long as
three or four of them, and he had been a great soft cuddly creature. This thing had a man's blood
all over its face, but it was shaped like Bobo. She checked for a fraction of a second too long, and
the wolf sprang over her and round behind her. She turned with it and caught it with her axe as it
came down.

The wolf Perry had clipped leapt on him. Its claws sank in under his arm, between the leather of his
corslet and his body, and there they jammed. It thrashed to wrench its paw free and savaged him while
it did. Perry was wounded, and worse than that, he was weary. He had loaded himself with far too much
gear, and he had not known it until now, when the weight of it dragged at every movement.

Makheneb saw Perry in danger. He spun round and loosed. The arrow went clean through the wolf's head
and out the other side. Perry went on shaking it for a moment before he understood that what he was
shaking was dead weight, and then it was only a matter of untangling himself.

Marigold called out to all of them that these were only dogs; they need not worry. Lily went in
screaming beside her. "Think of it as a haystack!" She was pretending she was back on the farm.
Whatever they had told themselves, it put heart into the others. Már, who was in a fury by now,
came in with her axe raised high above her head. The blow was slow and plain to see, and the wolf
she aimed it at did not move in time. It was the one Makheneb had shot first. The axe came down and
it lay still.

Perry went to Findemir and tried to do something for him. He knew nothing about wounds. While he knelt
there, the great wolf came for him and clamped its jaws on his arm.

It had him fast and could not turn to meet anyone else. Makheneb took the shot. To hit a wolf in the
head is hard enough; to hit it in the head without harming the arm in its mouth is harder. Perry felt
the jaws slacken, and then begin to tighten again, and he tore his arm out; and the jaws snapped shut
on nothing and stayed shut. The arrow had gone into its head.

## After the wolves

The first thing was Findemir. Lily had a healing balm in her pack. She knew nothing about healing
either, but Makheneb did, and with the balm and his help she cleaned and bound the wounds. They were
bad. But Findemir was breathing, and he would not die. His eyes flickered. He could not sit up or
speak, and his breath came shallow.

Perry's wound had stopped bleeding. He had packed it with mud. He was not sure whether that was good
or bad.

It was not yet noon. Moriel had said the place Findemir was bound for lay a day and a half from
Barad Tarminalë, and they were only a few hours out. They could be back at the fort in half a day.
They agreed quickly: safety first, and introductions after.

Findemir could not ride, and so Már built a sledge. It lay low to the ground behind a horse, and she
bent saplings over it so that they sprang and cushioned it as it rocked, and though the ground was
rough the litter hardly moved. It was very fine work. Perry rode Leaper back.

They reached the gate of Barad Tarminalë and shouted. "Good news! We found him! He's alive!"

## The evening at the fort

Moriel took Findemir in at once and saw to his wounds.

Már had questions of her own. The others knew Findemir only by hearsay; they had come looking for
him. She had been with him, and yet she knew almost as little. He had talked to her about his errand,
and she had understood hardly any of it. He had needed to find something. There was some kind of
mission, and she was supposed to be going along with him. Nobody had ever told her why. "They sent
me with him and told me nothing," she said. "I do everything for them, and they tell me nothing."

Lily was over the worst of her shock, and she did what she knew how to do. She put her innkeeper's
manner back on and made supper, and opened the preserves Goldberry had given them, and passed round
her flask of liquor. It had been a long day. She told Moriel and Már about Bree and the ill feeling
there, and how they had come looking for Findemir because they needed help. She did not mention Tom.
None of them did. They did not understand Tom themselves, and they did not know how they would
explain him to anyone else.

Lily set her spear in a pot of boiling water to take the frost out of it. It worked; the frost went.
But the frost left something behind. Etched into the metal, like the pattern on frosted glass, was an
after-image of it, and it would not come off.

Perry sat with Moriel and asked her everything she could tell him about treating wounds. He had
realised that he was wholly incompetent at it. She told him the mud had not been the right move,
though it was not always the wrong one; mostly you should wash it. Perry took a great deal of this in.
He had some ideas of his own, such as giving a man who was losing blood some of his own. Moriel said
that was a lesson for a good deal later. In a few generations, said Lily, the Oakroots might get
there.

Makheneb had gathered up his arrows after the fight. They were covered in the wolves' blood, and he
sat and studied them. Long ago, when he was young, he had gone on an expedition into the southern
forest of Mirkwood while the Necromancer still held Dol Guldur. That was the worst place he had ever
been, and the worst thing he had ever felt. This was worse. The longer he looked at it, the more it
was like handling something that burned without heat. He snapped the arrow in his hands and threw it
in the fire. He said nothing to the others. He did not want to frighten them away.

Lily and Perry talked by the fire. "How are you doing?" she asked him.

"You ran in screaming," said Perry. "That was impressive. Though I found the haystack confusing."

"Thank you," said Lily. "It turns out it was not a haystack."

Perry asked what they made of the Dwarf they had picked up. She had simply fallen in with them, and
they with her. She had not had much choice. Perry had been half-conscious on his horse for most of
the way back, and had hardly seen her.

It was a quiet evening, and in the course of it the five of them began, a little, to choose one
another. Perry felt he owed Makheneb for the shot that had taken the wolf off him, and he wanted to
be in the good graces of a traveller who had seen so much more of the world. Már found herself
watching out for Marigold. She was used to being the smallest in any company, and here she was not
the smallest. Lily was still worried for Perry, and a little guilty that she had not been able to
keep her own cousin from harm. And Marigold took to Lily, who was curious about everything and
always caring for somebody, and was up at five in the morning like herself.

## Moriel's charge

Late in the evening Moriel took Marigold aside. She had Findemir's satchel, and she laid out what was
in it on the table: a book bound in leather, a map, and some other things.

"Findemir will need a great deal of care," she said. "I expect I'll be here tending him for a while.
It seems he was working on something that can't wait. I know you were attacked, and I don't say this
lightly. But I need you to finish what he started."

She hesitated. "This isn't something that's shared outside the Dúnedain. But I trust you, in a way I
can't quite explain."

She unrolled the map and went over it with Marigold. They had been heading up into the downs, north
of here. On the map was something Marigold had never seen marked on any map: ruins, underneath what
looked like a natural cavern, inside the cavern itself.

People speak of the First Age and the Second and the Third, Moriel said; the Third is the present
count of years, the last few thousand. These ruins went back to the First Age, and the records about
them were very incomplete. Marigold had never heard the word *Númenórean*. Moriel explained that the
Númenóreans were the people who had lived in these lands many thousands of years ago. Some of their
ruins lay underground now. There were whole cities hidden away, with the earth risen over them, and
cities under the sea.

This place was a very important one, and it needed tending on a schedule. The journal set out the
instructions: how to get in, and what to do once inside. There were two levels. One could be called
the outside of the front door, and the other the inside of the house, short of the inner vestibule.
On each level there was what Moriel called an artifact, though it was not a single thing. It was part
of the building, a working piece of it, and it had to be kept whole and clean and working. If it was
broken, or had pieces missing, that had to be put right.

It sounded to Marigold like being asked to go through an old empty warehouse and make sure the lamps
worked on every floor. The journal was clear enough. Its language was odd: it read as if it were a
couple of hundred years old, though the instructions were plainly recent. The map was clear too, and
it led to the same place they had been making for.

"Here," Moriel said, and put the whole of it into Marigold's hands. "Once you're there, it should
take you a day at the most."

"We will carry on this mission," said Marigold. "You won't regret trusting us."

Moriel hugged her.

## The blizzard

Marigold woke the others at five in the morning. "We've got work to do," she said, and spread the map
out, and told them. It was a big responsibility. It was also, she thought, pretty easy. They could
certainly handle it.

It was the direction they had been going anyway, a day and a half away. Lily said that Tom and
Goldberry had told them to help Findemir, and this was helping Findemir. They would need to learn a
song for wolves, Perry said, next time they saw Tom. Már felt that she owed it to Findemir; she had
been given this task in all but name when she was sent with him.

Some of it did not quite make sense to her. If this was only a matter of going into a warehouse and
seeing that the lamps worked, why had her clan sent someone whose work was the dead? The thought did
not come into her head.

They camped the first night. On the second day it grew noticeably colder as they climbed. It was not
a mountain they were climbing, only higher hills, a few of them standing shoulder to shoulder; but by
the time they came near the place where the cavern should be, they were riding into a full blizzard.

They could see very little. It felt unnatural, or at least unseasonable. It might have been only a
freak storm. None of them had been this far north before, and none of them knew what was usual here.
They were very cold. Everyone had put on every layer they owned, with blankets over the top. Már
rode behind Makheneb on Strawberry, the sprightliest of their mounts.

Makheneb asked Marigold whether the journal gave any sense of how urgent this was. It did. The work
had to be done under a certain moon, within a month at most, or perhaps half a month. They did not
know where they stood in that window. An hour or a day would make no difference, but they could not
go away for a couple of weeks and come back.

Perry was worried that if they pressed on now they would go in the wrong direction. His maps did not
reach this far north. They agreed to look for shelter, and if there was none, to push on.

Lily found it. Something told her to go higher, though going higher meant more wind. At the top of
one of the hills there were ruins: a large circle of cobbles, broken up, with a low wall round parts
of it and a ring of pillars, most of them long since fallen. The wall was about three feet high, and
between the wall and the drifts it made a good break from the wind, with room for a fire. No one had
been there. The snow might have hidden the signs, but there were no other signs at all.

They sat out the storm there for a few hours. It was not quite nightfall when the blizzard died, and
the last of the sun came out, though the valley below was already partly in shadow.

## The cave

Perry kept watch on the way they had come, in case the wolves were tracking them; this was wolf
country. Now that the air had cleared he could see a very long way south, and he could see the
change from winter into spring across miles of land.

Makheneb looked the other way, inward, down into a bowl among the hills. Three of the great wolves
were walking in single file out of a patch of shadow. He looked again and saw that the shadow was
the mouth of a cavern. The wolves were leaving it. He was several hundred feet above them and a long
way off, and the wind was blowing from them to him. He followed them with his eyes as they wound up
between two of the far foothills and into a glade, half a mile off and still going. Then they were
gone.

He climbed down and told the others. "Three of the big ones. They were leaving."

The place matched the documents. It was the only cave they had seen.

"I wouldn't trust them to stay gone," said Makheneb.

The journal said the work had last been done about forty years ago. It said nothing about wolves. But
a great deal can happen in forty years.

They decided to scout it while the light held. The entrance was wide, perhaps forty feet across, with
a low overhang of frozen snow across the front of it, so that from outside it looked like a low, wide
slot. Inside, the rock went up in a great arch, thirty feet high or more. Már and Makheneb stayed
outside to keep watch, and the other three crept in. Perry had so many pots and pans hung about him
that he rang like a tinker's cart, and somebody had to reach over and still them.

At first it was a cave like any other, with earth and pebbles underfoot, and bones, and scat. By the
light of Perry's lantern they could see one side where the wolves plainly lay up and slept, though
none were there now. Then the cave began to wind, curving slowly round to the right and sloping
gently down. About the length of a great hall in, the floor under their feet turned to cobbles. The
cave was natural, but this was not.

They could not see where it ended. They went back for the others.

## A night of traps

There was a long argument. If they went down now, they might finish before the wolves came back, but
they would be hurrying, and they would have to come out the same way, perhaps into the wolves, and
perhaps hurt. If they waited, they could learn the wolves' habits, but that would waste time. And
had the wolves only been passing through? The den inside said otherwise. It was not one day's worth
of bones.

In the end they settled on traps. They would give it one night at most. If the wolves came back, the
traps would tell them. If not, they would go down in the morning.

Lily and Marigold went out for materials and came back again and again with armloads of dead branches
and kindling, and pine boughs, which burn well. Már set to work on a pit in the mouth of the cave,
set sharpened stakes in the bottom of it, and laid pine branches over the top. They piled kindling
beside it so that a torch would set it all ablaze. Makheneb asked whether they could make fire-arrows
so that no one would need to run in with a torch, and it was easily done: a rag, a little of Lily's
liquor, and the lantern.

While he waited, Makheneb felt out the thing below them. Evil has flavours, as spices do. To someone
who cannot bear spice, all of it is simply hot; to someone who has grown used to it, one heat is quite
unlike another. This place had a deep foreboding in it, but it was different from Dol Guldur. It
intrigued him. He did not feel better prepared for it. He felt drawn. He had found the trail, and
this was the place. However frightening that was, this was where he wanted to be, and this was the
thing he wanted to hunt.

They waited on the hillside downwind of the cave, near enough to strike quickly. Lily went up to check
on the horses. They would rather have been somewhere warmer, but they were keeping close together for
the heat, and they were content.

The night passed. The wolves did not come back.

## Down

The morning was bright and cold, the kind of cold that has a crystal quality in the sunshine. There
was no hot breakfast.

They left the horses in the ruins on the hilltop, which was the safest place for them, tied with the
kind of knot a horse can pull free if it has to. If they went in and did not come out, the horses
would not be left to starve. If anything happened to Strawberry, Makheneb said, he was never going
back; he would take the road west to the Grey Havens instead. Lily kissed every one of the animals on
the forehead.

Then they went down. The way sloped gently and kept on sloping. They kept to the pace of the shortest
legs among them and went as quietly as they could. It was like going down a very wide, very slow
spiral stair with no stairs. It took about an hour.

At the bottom the passage opened into a chamber so large that their light did not reach its walls.
Lily went back up the passage a couple of hundred feet, to where she could still see the others'
lights, and there she put out her own lantern and kept watch in the dark, listening back up the way
they had come.

In the middle of the floor was a wheel of stone. It was set into the floor and stood about four feet
high, round, and some eight feet across, and a line of symbols ran round it. Marigold took out the
journal and compared the two. She found where the phrase began and followed it round.

Two pieces were missing, like two slices out of a pie, and the gaps broke the phrase. One of them lay
on the ground beside the wheel. Marigold lifted it and fitted it back into its place. The other was
not anywhere near.

Looking closely at the edges, they saw the marks of a chisel. Someone had set out to break the
wheel on purpose, and not long ago. It had been done within the last year.

One of them had seen something glint against the far wall as they came in, and turned a lantern
that way. There were doors: a pair of them, huge, faced with a skin of metal worked in filigree. The
design was abstract and flowing and graceful, and the style of it was utterly strange to all of them.
The doors did not look as though they had been opened in a long time.

"That is not Dwarf-work," said Már. "Not any kind."

Somebody asked whether they could open it. Somebody else said, "Slow down." They did not know what
was on the other side.

They went round the walls of the chamber instead, all together, with the lanterns. Near the wall, not
far from the doors, they found a skeleton, and it was holding the missing piece.

## The belt

It had been dead a long while; there was nothing left of it but bones. Nothing had eaten it. It had
not been mauled. It looked human, and its clothes had not rotted at all. There was a weapon beside it,
and some other things.

Perry looked at it without touching it, and remembered.

A year and a half ago, perhaps two, there had been a very shady-looking man outside Bree, asking a
great many questions. Perry had pulled him aside at the gate and was thinking of taking it further,
and then Perry's superior had come over and said, *I'll take care of this.* When Perry followed it up
afterwards, the answers he got did not make sense. They implied that nothing had really happened, and
that the man had gone on his way without hindrance. Perry had never had a good opinion of the man he
answered to at the gate, though he was not one to hold a poor opinion of anybody, and he had kept it
down. He supposed his superior must have his reasons.

That man had worn a braided belt of blue and white. Perry had never seen another like it. And here it
was, on the skeleton.

"This is him," said Perry. "Do you remember, a year and a half back? This man asking all sorts of
questions outside town?"

Lily had heard about it from some of the farmsteads. It had become something of an issue.

As Perry described him, the man took shape before them in the air: a shadowy form, in the likeness of
the man he remembered.

Every lantern went out.

## The ghost

Up the passage, Lily had let her eyes grow used to the dark. She had been watching the others' lights,
and all at once the lights went out, every one of them. She called out: "What's going on over there?"

What came back was shouting and shrieking.

In the chamber it was black. There was a thing in the dark with them, and they could not see it. Már
knew the dead as well as anyone living, and this was not like the dead she knew. She was a long way
from home ground. But she knew one thing about it. It had put out the lanterns because it hated light
and fire. That was a thing they might use.

"Follow my voice," someone called. "Come to me. We'll get together so we can feel we're together, and
put our backs to each other."

They groped their way together. Makheneb struck his lantern alight again. The light blinded him for a
moment, and when his eyes opened the ghost was right in front of him. Its hand closed over the flame,
and as it drew back the flame went out again. But he had seen it, if only for an instant, and he had
an arrow ready. The arrow passed through it with an unnatural, shrill sound, and then it was dark
again.

Már set herself to guard the lantern that was left. And when the light came again and Már saw the thing in it, she had had enough. She swung her axe with all
her strength behind it and cleaved through the ghost. They heard the crunch of the skeleton behind it
as the blade went on through. There was a howl that seemed to suck the air out of the room, and then
silence.

They lit the lanterns. There was nothing there but the crushed and cloven bones. Everyone stood very
still for a long moment.

Then they took the missing piece from the skeleton's hands, carried it back to the wheel, and fitted
it in. There were two layers to the stone. The lower one was unharmed, and the piece rested on it
securely, and the carving ran whole round the wheel again.

As it settled into place, they heard a soft sound. The great doors had opened, a little way, inward.
