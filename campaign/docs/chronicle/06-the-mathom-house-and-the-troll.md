## The Mathom-house

They lingered a day in Michel Delving before going west. Már and Lily went off about the town on
business of their own, and the other three spent the day as they pleased.

Hobbit hospitality, Makheneb found, meant food, and then more food. It was the kind of food that
does not make you want to walk ten miles afterwards, and does make you want a nap. The hobbits were
uncomfortable with an Elf, and being uncomfortable made them overly polite, and being overly polite
made them serve him. With the Men of Bree they were more at ease, and a little more distrustful.
They knew Bree. Still, the Company was with Marigold, and that helped.

The Mathom-house is something like a jumble sale, if all the things were never sold, and were kept
all year round in a building of their own. Nothing in it was for sale. It was all heirlooms and
things of note, and *of note* was very generously understood. This was important because it had
been someone's grandfather's; that was important because it was the only left-handed toaster. It
was not all rubbish. There were hand-made watches, and old paintings, and a great deal of furniture.
There was very little that was truly valuable, but a great deal that mattered to someone. There was
a replica of the original golf club. The original had been borrowed, sadly, and that was why the
rules about borrowing were so strict now.

Perry spent hours copying the flower map. It was kept by Malva Slowfoot, who called herself the
curator, though there was no sign that anything had ever been turned away. The Mathom-house was her
little fiefdom. She was very glad that he was copying the map and not asking to borrow it, because a
great many people came asking to borrow things, and she did not like it. *This is not a library. It
is a Mathom-house.* It also helped that Firework, the guard dog, a tiny white terrier whose bark was
very much worse than its bite, had taken a shine to Perry. It lay curled up asleep on his feet while
he worked. Perry loves dogs.

He had been at it a couple of hours, and was well into it, when he noticed a hobbit child standing
right at his elbow.

"Oh, dear. What's your name?"

"Primula. Are you an artist?"

"Sometimes."

"Are you done with that? I need to borrow it."

"You need to borrow it? What for?"

"I can't tell you."

"Is it secret mushroom stuff?"

"I can't tell you."

"Well," said Perry, "I think you could borrow this map. The copy I'm making. That'll work just as well."

She scrunched up her face. "I don't think Mr Bilbo will like that. But we can try." She said it was
Bilbo Baggins she was fetching it for. "Don't tell Malva I'm here. It's a secret."

"You're very sneaky to have got past Malva," said Perry. "I'll reward that. I won't tell."

Perry is no artist. His copy was a great deal less detailed than the original. He kept the things he
cared about, the roads and the lie of the land, so that you could tell how to get from one place to
another. He left out the mushrooms. There was a mushroom in the legend and not one on the map. Since
that was very likely the whole reason Primula and Mr Bilbo wanted it, they were going to be greatly
disappointed.

Once Malva came by and called Firework to her, and Perry realised a moment too late that the child did
not want to be seen. He turned, and Primula stepped out from behind an old dresser as though nothing
had happened. She had dodged it. Perry made a second copy, a little better than the first, gave her
one, and she scurried off with it as soon as it was in her hand. The other he stuck to the edge of his
grandfather's map with a bit of tape, so that it hung off the side where there was a good deal of empty
space. It took about two minutes to unfold.

## Apples and tanneries

Makheneb had two errands. He wanted a treat for Strawberry, and he wanted someone to refit his armour.
It was leather armour, and it had been made for someone else.

On the edge of town there were fruit trees everywhere, on people's land and leaning out over the paths.
Branches over the path were plainly anybody's. As for the rest, Makheneb could not see how anyone could
own a tree. He picked apples carefully, so as not to damage anything. He hopped a fence to get at the
best of them, tried one, found it delicious, and took three more, since he ought to think of the other
horses too, and turned to go. The farmer was standing there watching him. Makheneb waved. The farmer
was too stunned to speak or to stop him. It was not that the Elf had been sneaky about it. He had simply
solved a small problem, openly. They would talk about the sheer audacity of it for generations.

The armour was harder. There were no shops in the Shire, in the way there were in Bree, apart from a
few places like the inn. You were expected to know where things were, or whom to ask. Farms were easy
to find; services happened in people's houses. Makheneb tried following his nose for a tannery, which
he felt sure he could smell from a long way off. He went out of town and back in again several times and
found nothing.

Perry had given up his breastplate to the long-folk, and he was going about Michel Delving in a shirt,
a pair of short trousers with a great many pockets, and a pair of clogs. None of that had felt dangerous
in the Shire. He did not know how it would go on the road, where a pointed enough bramble might take
him out. He too went looking for a tanner, and found none. He and Makheneb passed each other in the
street three times.

## Melody Took

Marigold had gone looking for books, and in particular for storybooks, and she had found a great many.
The Mathom-house's book-room was all narrow gaps between shelves, with books piled on the floor three
feet high. She had made one pile she had been through and another she was still working through.

The first thing she found was a little book of a hundred pages or so, a revised and condensed collection
of stories by a hobbit named Melody Took. They were tales of strange creatures and strange sightings: a
two-headed cow, magic done by an Elf, and one in which Gandalf appeared, apparently to help someone cheat
in a cart race. And in one of them there was a single, sideways mention of the long-folk of the North
Moors.

She kept digging, and at last she found what the little book had been made from: seven old journals in
Melody Took's own tight hand. Melody had gone about asking not only a great many hobbits but a great many
people passing through for their stories, and she had written down what they told her. Some of it was
plainly someone pulling her leg. All of it had been set down faithfully, exactly as it was told, so that
the quality of the tales varied wildly, but the breadth of them was astonishing. It looked like forty or
fifty years of work. The first volume never left the Eastfarthing; Melody had been finding her feet with
local lore. Then she had found a knack for it and a taste for it, and the later volumes went further
afield, with travellers passing through and people she went to meet. She had died in her late eighties,
and the seventh volume stopped a third of the way through.

Her handwriting was hard to read. Marigold had got to the fourth volume without finding the long-folk
story she was sure must be in there somewhere when Malva came by.

"I'm sorry, dear. You'll need to put the books away. You can come back tomorrow. We open between first and
second breakfast."

"Can I borrow it?"

Marigold was a hobbit, which helped. It did not help enough. "That will be quite enough," said Malva. "It
will be here in the morning. Please put the book down. Firework and I will walk you out."

Marigold went out looking sadly back at the books.

A little hobbit boy was waiting to one side of the door with a conspiratorial gleam in his eye. "She kick
you out?"

"She kicked me out."

"We've got to wait till the dog has its dinner," he said. "That's how we got in last night. I'm Ammie."

"I'm Marigold."

They sat on a bench by the square to wait. Marigold watched Perry and Makheneb pass each other twice more,
looking sadder each time, and at last she called them over.

"How are you doing, bud?"

They asked her whether she knew anywhere good to eat, and whether she knew of a tanner. Any tanner, she
told them, would be downstream. The river did not run through Michel Delving. The tanning was done down in
the Eastfarthing.

"Where's the stream? I haven't found a single stream here."

They went to get something to eat. After dinner, Marigold said, she had plans.

## The inn

When they got back to the inn, Már and Lily had a small crowd around them in one corner. Már had had more
than a few, and was telling stories with more enthusiasm than the others had ever seen in her. They were
not tales of the Company's adventures, but tales from before, things Már had seen or heard. Some of them
were very interesting, and some were probably complete nonsense. Now and then Lily chimed in with a story
of her own, and Perry, eating his dinner, was surprised by them. At the gate of Bree he had watched people
come and go, but he had never really got to know them or hear their stories. Lily had. She had a great
wealth of them, mostly from the east and the south, from the Men who traded up and down the roads.

Makheneb sat and made arrows. He still had the Númenórean arrowheads from the ruins, and he fitted them to
new shafts and bound them. The innkeeper kept bringing him a fresh drink and lingering, and the third time
he asked whether he could try the great bow. Makheneb took him out the back. The bow was almost as tall as
the innkeeper, and it was like handing a bow to a small child. He could not draw it. He said he had had
a better reach when he was younger.

Perry had a few drinks, since Már was having a few, and went round asking people on their own whether they
had any experience with a needle. He had an idea: trousers whose legs could be tied on below his short ones
and taken off again. Nobody at the inn could help, but somebody knew who could. Salvina was a marvel at all
kinds of needlework; she could surely manage it. She would be in bed by now, but here were the directions,
and he should go in the morning after a pleasant breakfast and just ask politely. Perry thanked him and
bought him a drink, since he insisted.

Then Perry taught some of them a card game, and they taught him theirs, all of them trick-taking games and
little variations of one another, and the evening turned into a tournament. Perry swung wildly between
losing badly and suddenly sweeping everything off the table. Beginner's luck, they called it, but he also
made choices so idiotic that they became genius. By the end he had three or four new friends. He did not
want anything from them. He did give them an open invitation to Bree, and he wrote a letter to his niece
and nephew about his travels and left it with one of them, a hobbit named Posco. Posco was going to the
Eastfarthing and could get it that far, and then see that it found its way to Bree.

"I haven't seen my family in a long time," said Perry.

"I haven't seen mine in almost two weeks," said Posco.

Perry asked whether he could bring Posco anything back from the west. Berries, said Posco, and mushrooms,
if Perry had extras. They had tried growing them in the garden, but those things needed an unruly sort of
place and gardens were too orderly. And the children always got to the wild ones round Frogmorton before
the grown-ups knew they were ripe. The children there were resourceful. They were also devious. Posco had
been one of them himself.

Perry mentioned the child he had met at the Mathom-house, Primula, and that she had been fetching a map for
Bilbo.

"The Brandybuck girl? What's Bilbo up to now?" Posco shook his head. Bilbo had had his adventure and come
back wealthier than anyone, and now he put ideas in children's heads. Dragons, and Dwarves (no offence to
the one in the corner), and trolls and Elves and wizards. It was nothing but trouble for the young. At least
at Posco's age you could hear those stories without feeling you had to go off into the wild.

## The heist

After dark Marigold went back to the Mathom-house, and Ammie was waiting.

Round the back there was a pile of boxes, precariously stacked: a big wooden crate at the bottom and a
tool chest on top. Ammie scrambled up and jumped, and caught the edge of a small window that was mostly
there to let the air in. Its frame had been bent so far that it was barely attached. He pulled himself
through. "It's in here!"

Marigold followed. She could not get a grip at first. Then she found the edge of a shelf inside and began
to drag herself through, slowly, and realised as she went that the shelf was not fixed to anything, not to
the wall and not to the shelves on either side. She could push on through the window and fall on her face,
or go carefully and risk bringing the whole shelf down, or back out. She pushed on. She overshot. It was
about six feet down to a rough carpet, and she landed on her nose. She kept herself from crying out. Her
nose bled, and it might be broken, and it hurt a great deal, but nobody came.

Ammie came and patted her face in the dark. "Your nose is still there." It was a bit bent, and he gave it
a sharp tug, and it snapped back into place. Field medicine in the hands of a hobbit child.

"Do you know what you're getting?" he asked.

"Yes. Do you?"

He led her through the house, down narrow passages between furniture stacked almost to the ceiling, up
creaking stairs past the bookshelves, and up to the next floor, where there was a bust made of marble.

"That's my grandma," said Ammie.

It was too dark to read the name on it. The two of them might just carry it between them. It would fit out
of the window, but getting it out safely would be another matter. It could go out of the front door, if the
door were open.

"We could come back tomorrow," said Marigold, "and take it out the front."

She tried lifting it. She could carry it alone, but only the way you carry something very heavy across a
room and then must put it down at once. It would break her toes if she dropped it. They carried it down
together towards the front door, Marigold taking the weight while Ammie told her where to go. But he could
not make up his mind where to put it, and she had to put it down somewhere, now. So she set it a few paces
inside the door and put an open book in front of it, so that it looked as though the bust were sitting there
reading.

By then Marigold was so nervous about the bust that she had forgotten all about the journals. They made their
plan. Tomorrow, when the Mathom-house opened, Ammie would go in and start crying and screaming to draw
everyone to him, and Marigold would take the bust out the front door. Then they left by the door.

## Breakfast, and Salvina

In the morning the Company ate breakfast together. The cooking in the Shire is modest but plentiful and well
seasoned: potatoes with sage and rosemary and a great deal of butter. They meant to leave at midday.

Marigold felt the others out, carefully. How did they feel about rules? How did they feel about children?
About security? About heavy things? They did not know what to make of it. If a crime was bad enough, Makheneb
said, he usually just killed it. Perry told her what Posco had said about Bilbo sending children off on
errands. Marigold, who had grown up on stories of Bilbo and who wanted adventures of her own, was a little
nervous at that. "No," she said, "Bilbo's really cool. The kids are good. The kids are good."

Then Perry went to see Salvina. Her hobbit-hole sat in the crook of a little dip in the land, one of two or
three homes that faced one another round it. Her door was wide open on a fine day, and she had set herself up
just inside it, out of the direct sun but in the breeze. Someone was hanging out washing.

"Can I help you?"

"Salvina? My name is Perry. I'm only passing through, but someone at the inn sent me to you." He showed her
the shorts. They were the only ones he had, which was why he wanted them to be as versatile as possible. "I
spend a good deal of time on the road, and falling down in the woods, and I'm worried about what bites.
Could these be extended? And untied again, to make them shorts?"

She had nothing that would fit him as it was. She hurried in and came out with several kinds of fabric,
most of them bags of some sort, from something like a potato sack at one end to something like a pillowcase
at the other, and held them up against him and looked him over. "Do you have another pair of trousers?"

He had not thought to bring any. She and the woman hanging washing conferred, and came back with a very long
dress. He could wrap it around himself, sideways, for decency, while she worked on his shorts. They gave him
a room and a chair. He had to hold the dress in place the whole time.

He offered to pay. She thanked him and said that if he would sit and talk with her while she worked, that
would be payment enough. What she wanted was gossip.

Perry is not a man who tells stories about himself. His work was to watch and to listen. But he gave her
something: everyone in the Company was jealous of Strawberry. The rest of them rode old ponies and old
horses, and there was Makheneb on Strawberry, like something out of a song, and the very leaves seemed to part
for her. The others had been proposing schemes: persuading Makheneb to trade, or stealing her in the night and
pretending she was lost, so that whoever found her first would get to ride her.

## The second heist

Makheneb had nothing in particular to do that morning. He did not trust whatever Marigold was up to, and so he
followed her, quietly.

Ammie found Marigold near the Mathom-house. "Are you ready?"

It was busier than usual, and Malva was greeting people at the door. Ammie went in first. Marigold went in a
little after him, so that they would not seem to be together. Makheneb slipped in round her while she stood
looking awkward and suspicious by the entrance, and tucked himself into a nook further inside.

From there he saw it. Up on the half-landing, among the bookshelves, there was a ladder that slid along a
rail, and Ammie was at the top of it. Ammie fell. It looked like a strange sort of fall, and then Makheneb saw
that the boy's leg had caught in the rungs, and he heard it snap, and the scream that followed was real.

Makheneb did not use the stairs. He caught the banister and was up at the boy's side at once. The leg was
broken badly, with the bone out through the skin. He drew his knife and slit the trouser leg open. To
everyone looking up from below, there was suddenly an Elf with a drawn knife standing over a screaming child.

Firework came yapping. Malva came up and saw what he was doing, and as he looked about for something to use as
a splint, she clucked and handed him a broomstick instead, and helped him. She apologised over and over. "I'll
make a sign," she said, "so that children don't climb the ladder." Makheneb stopped the bleeding, set the leg
against the broomstick, and bound it tight with strips of cloth. The boy would have to be carried. Was there a
doctor? There were a couple of people who were good with healing, Malva said, mostly with animals. She led the
way.

Marigold, meanwhile, had picked up the bust. At the front there is a little step, only a few inches high, but a
few inches is enough when you are not expecting it. She stumbled. The bust slipped out of her hands and came down
on her left foot.

The pain was blinding. She tipped the bust off her foot. At least two of her toes were broken. The bust had landed
flat side down, and she did not think it had broken, though a chip might have come off. The bust was her first
concern, and she kept quiet. She could still walk on her heel. She hobbled across the road with it, to a gap in a
fence where a little trail went off into a field, and set it down in the long grass, where nobody would see it
unless they were right on top of it. Then she sat down and had a good cry about her toes.

When she had finished, she saw Malva leading Makheneb away down the street with the boy in his arms. The
Mathom-house was empty. Even the dog was gone. She limped back in, went straight to the books, took the sixth
volume of Melody Took's journals, and hid it under her shirt.

## Odo Boffin

The healer was a few doors down: Odo Boffin, a barber-surgeon and animal doctor. There are two sorts of healer.
One makes poultices and good broths that help you feel better. The other says *no, that's on backwards, let's set
it right,* or cuts it off. Odo was the second sort. There was a hacksaw hanging on his back wall, and a great
sling hanging from the roof for holding up a pony or a cow. He was grateful this had not happened in calving
season.

By the end of the morning Makheneb was a minor celebrity in Michel Delving: the Elf who had saved the child with
such quick action, and where had he learned those techniques?

Marigold came limping in as he was leaving. Her toes were already badly swollen and purple.

"You'll never believe what happened," said Makheneb. "A child fell. They may never walk again. Right through the
leg. I thought that was supposed to be one of the strongest bones in the body."

Ammie lay on the table while Odo looked at him. His eyes were red and his cheeks flushed from crying, but he was
spent now, and calmer. He looked over at Marigold.

"Did you get it?"

Marigold nodded. Makheneb saw that too.

Odo looked at her foot. "I was skipping rocks," said Marigold, "and I dropped a really big one."

He had her sit and put the foot up. He enlisted Makheneb to help, and cleaned it and cooled it until the swelling
began to go down. As far as he could tell, he was able to work the bones into a position where they should heal
correctly. Then he bandaged it until she had a great club of a foot, and told her firmly to stay off it for a
couple of weeks, and to have the dressing changed every three or four days.

She whispered to Ammie where she had left the bust. If he wanted it moved again, that was his problem.

Leaning on Makheneb on the way back to the inn, she told him she was fine. She could ride. She had been worse.
"That is one brave child," she said. Makheneb said that Odo had been aghast to hear what ideas somebody had put in
the boy's head.

## The road west

The Company set out west at midday, all five of them. Perry was the guide. He still had Tom's honeycomb for
Círdan, and he now had trousers whose legs he could tie on and off, and he was no longer holding up a dress.

They left the main roads. Here and there they rode on the broken remains of old roads, not kept up but not gone,
wandering off in strange directions; the way they followed west was a footpath, well trodden, that had never been
made into a road. They met a few travellers, fewer than on the roads round Bree, and some of them on foot, whom
they passed.

At one place the old broken cobbles turned north while their path went on west, and at the fork there were two
men standing by the road talking. Something about them struck Marigold as wrong. She could not have said what;
there was nothing plain to see, and they were not showing any weapons. She only knew she did not want to ride
right past them. "Keep an eye out, guys," she said, and she led them wide. They slowed down and turned off early,
went across country, overshot, and came back to the path well beyond the men. It wearied them. That night they
made a cold camp, with no fire.

Marigold's foot throbbed. She could not lie flat with it raised; she was on a horse with it hanging down, and she
could feel her pulse in her toes. It was not bad, but it was obnoxious. She had brought a book of home remedies
with her, and there were two or three plants in it that looked promising, to keep the wound clean and bring the
swelling down, if they could be made into a paste. She and Makheneb went off from camp to look for them. It was
the root that mattered, and the book only showed the plant in flower, so it took them some time to recognise it.
It was later and darker than they liked by the time they found it. While the others made camp and cooked and saw
to the bedrolls, the two of them brought the roots back, washed them, boiled them, and pounded them into a paste,
pale green like horseradish. Marigold undid her bandages. There was her furry foot, and her big squashed purple
toes, and she packed the green paste in between them and bound it all up again and hoped for the best.

## A bird

Out in the foothills, Makheneb asked the others to let him make a detour.

There was a kind of bird in these parts, a great raptor of the size of a turkey vulture, with a wingspan of seven
or eight feet. They were not hard to kill so much as rarely seen; around Mirkwood they almost never strayed so
far that an Elf got the chance. They were a delicacy. Makheneb had known the great Eagles at the Battle of Five
Armies, and knew them for a whole people, and these birds were no kin of theirs, whatever the Eagles might say
about lesser birds. They did not swoop down on their prey. They seized it, flew up, and dropped it to its death,
and then fed on it. Mostly they took sheep. Marigold had heard of them in the stories that hobbits tell children to
keep them from running off into the woods: a bird that might grab a young hobbit and fly away with it.

Makheneb's first argument was that it was the best food there was. His second was that he was very hungry. The
second was more convincing.

He and Perry hunted it together. Marigold did not realise until afterwards how carefully the two of them had kept
her busy, and kept an eye on her. She had been the bait. The bird had come for her. Makheneb's arrow brought it
down, and a clean stroke through the neck finished it. That evening at camp nobody undersold it or oversold it. It
was delicious, and there was plenty left over for the road.

## Elf-land

They crossed a hill and had a brief glimpse of the horizon, and there was the Gulf of Lhûn. Then they came down
into a valley. They could not yet see the Grey Havens, but they were near; it was a few days away at most.

Makheneb felt the change as they came into the valley. He had crossed into Lothlórien once, and he had come by
Rivendell on his way west. In the lands of the Elves everything feels old and still. Here it felt like a held
breath, and he did not know what it was being held for, or when it would be let go.

"We are entering Elf-land," he told the others. "We're getting close. Keep your wits about you. If we meet any of
my kin, let me make the introductions. You can talk to them; only be respectful."

The valley bottomed out in a great many streams, all running down to the gulf beside the big river to the north,
and there, where their path went, was a bridge. It was not Wood-elf work, but it was plainly Elvish, and it crossed
a creek still full from the spring floods. In Lórien, and near the Woodland Realm, the Elves always knew when
someone came. Here there was no sense of anyone at all. It was very strange.

Lily had gone ahead a couple of hours before to scout the way. Now they heard her bird-whistle, the one Makheneb had
taught them. It was a passable bird, but not the right one; a real Elf would have done it properly, so Makheneb
knew at once that it was Lily. He found her across the creek on the far side of the bridge, tucked down and trying
to stay hidden, wide-eyed, shaking her head and pointing at the bridge.

Makheneb made himself small and nocked an arrow, and crept round to see. Under the bridge, deep in its shadow, was a
troll.

It had Lily's horse. It had lashed Buddy to the roots under the bridge with vines, or something like vines, and it
was stacking stones to make a pit big enough to roast a horse over. Lily was hurt, and could not see any way to
get in and get Buddy out again.

Makheneb knew about trolls. This was a cave-troll, not a great chieftain of its kind, but very strong, with a hide
so tough that even arrows struggled to pierce it. They hate Dwarves above all things, and they are fiercest against
them. Nobody fights one alone unless they are very lucky or have something close to magic on their side. But they
are as stupid as rocks. You can sometimes simply trick them and talk them round. If they find out they have been
tricked, they get very angry, and conversations with trolls tend to end with the troll picking up the nearest stick
or stone and beating your head in. And trolls turn to stone in the sun.

Már went back into the trees with the horses. It was midday. The troll was in the shadow under the bridge, in a cut
about twenty feet deep, and to reach them in the sunlight it would have to come out of its shade.

They argued it out. Marigold wanted to talk to it; she was the least use in a fight anyway, with her foot. If it went
wrong, the others would shoot. Makheneb and Perry would lie hidden on either side of the bridge with arrows ready,
far enough away that the troll could not get at them. Perry needed all the help he could get to hit anything with a
bow, but he would have the first shot prepared. And they had something to bargain with: the Ranger rations they had
found in the ruins.

## Marigold and the troll

Marigold came limping up the path towards the bridge, as old and lame as she could make herself look, which was not
very hard. She stopped where the troll could see her but would have to work to throw anything at her. Makheneb had
given her his rope and grapnel for lowering things.

The troll sniffed the air, and caught sight of her.

"You're a bit small," it said. "And you stink of Elf."

"I know," said Marigold. "I want to get that smell off me. Thanks for telling me. It looks like you have a horse
there. I'm really struggling with my foot."

"You should come down and try the horse."

"I know a little bit about you," said Marigold. "I'm a little scared to come down and try the horse."

"The horse can't hurt you. The horse is secured."

"Well, I can't really walk down. So what if we trade? I have some food here. It was given to me by an old friend,
someone very clever and very powerful. And I gave some of it to an old friend of mine who was a troll and lived under
a bridge, and after he ate it he was able to walk out in the sunlight just like anyone else. It was incredible. I've
got some left over. You're so lucky. I'd trade it for the horse. But I need help. I'm so old I can hardly walk. Look
at my broken foot."

The troll looked at her with clear suspicion. "I'll try it," it said at last. "You don't look big enough to eat a
horse."

"I just need to get out of here. I don't want to stink of Elf like everything else round here."

"The horse stinks of Elf too."

"Then I'll have to wash it in a river. Is there one nearby?"

"You can come down to this creek." It stamped across the water and stood looking up at her, deep in the shadow of the
bridge.

"I'll lower this down once you untie the horse," said Marigold. "And once you've eaten it, you can bring the horse up
here for me. I've got more rations to pay you with."

"Eat the rations and then bring the horse up?"

"Take pity on this old woman. I can bring you more rations to help you walk into the light."

The troll tried to think that through. Then it waved a hand, as though she were making it all too complicated,
stamped over, pulled the lashings half off the horse, and stamped back. "All right. The horse is loose."

Marigold lowered the rations on the rope. They were in a satchel of waxed leather, a few small tightly wrapped bricks,
the kind you crumble into boiling water. The troll grabbed one and bit through it and ate half.

"As soon as you've finished it," said Marigold, "you can walk out in the light for three hours. So use it quickly."

They could see it trying to do sums. "Do I need to eat all of them to go in the sun?"

"One more bite and you're good."

It ate half of the second brick, leaving a brick and a half. Then it went over to Buddy and lifted him bodily onto its
shoulder, and took four steps out into the sunlight, and stopped.

It had turned to stone. Buddy was still up on its shoulder, six feet off the ground, on top of a troll made of stone.

"We're alive!"

Lily called out from where she was hiding. She could move, she said, but she thought she had broken a couple of ribs.
When the troll came out at her, Buddy had thrown her, and she had struck the side of the bridge. The troll had not
seen her, and she had crept away into the undergrowth. She came hobbling over, holding her side.

## Getting Buddy down

The troll's rope was probably rubbish tied in knots, and nobody trusted it. They made their own. Perry remembered the
slings the cow-doctors use to lift a cow off the ground, and they built something like that: a harness under the horse
and a system of ropes from the bridge above. Marigold, with her foot as it was, had to direct rather than haul, and
because she had to explain everything, the work took more care and more time, and came out better for it. The point
was not to lift Buddy so much as to guide his fall, so that he would not break a leg; a horse with a broken leg would
have to be put down. Már and Lily were at the bottom, the others above, and on the count of three the ones below lifted
and the ones above pulled. For a moment it seemed the ropes would not hold, and Buddy would drop. It was a controlled
drop, and he came down without breaking anything. Lily was hurt again in the doing of it, worse than before. It was
better, she thought, that it was her and not the horse.

Makheneb had left them to it. Trolls have hoards, and he wanted to find this one's. He followed the stream up a little
way, and found the troll's den by the stink, which was no stranger to him. He pushed in. There were rotted carcasses of
animals, and a couple of skeletons that had been people, and scraps of cloth and scraps of metal and shiny things stacked
in a corner. From one skeleton's finger he took a ring with a ruby in it. There was a skull with the lower part of its
face smashed in and some of its hair still on it, and round the skull was a circlet of gold. And there was a pile of coins
and bright things, enough for several trips.

He came back again and again, stinking, and every time he brought more treasure and left for more, and after a while he
was wearing the golden circlet, a little crooked, like a crown. The others were doing well enough with the pulleys that
they let him get on with it. There was treasure enough for each of them to have a share.

They camped that night on the bridge. It was easily defended, and they knew exactly where the threat was: it was stone,
beneath them.

## The Grey Havens

The next day they came to the Grey Havens.

It was late in the afternoon, and the sun was going down beyond the city and throwing the ships into silhouette. The
Havens are famous for their ships, and none of them had ever seen anything like them. As they came nearer, the
silhouettes became ships at quays, three or four great galleys, and one in a dry dock half built. Roads came in from the
north and the south to meet their own, and down from the north came great wagons carrying whole tree trunks. They passed
through fields and farmland, and came to a ring of settled country around the city, and inside that a city within the
city. The people they passed looked at them with curiosity, but not unkindly.

Makheneb searched his memory. The Grey Havens were unlike any other Elvish place. He had heard somewhere that not only
would he be welcome here, but that Men and Dwarves had come here too, now and then; perhaps because of the docks, the way
out to whatever lies beyond the sea. As people looked at the Company and did not stop them, the old worry about whether
they would be allowed to stay settled in him. There would be a part of this city where all of them were welcome, and they
would need no special leave.

For Perry and Lily and Marigold it was the biggest and grandest city they had ever seen. They knew what a boat was; they
had even heard of people going down a river in a boat. These were not boats. Some of them had boats on them. They were
houses on the water.

Perry wanted to be indoors at once. It was overwhelming to be out in the street. It was overwhelming to look at the sea.
He had found out about the sea two weeks ago and had been told in the same breath that it was forbidden to him. He was
half convinced that if he went near the water they would arrest him.

"Where are all the trees?" he asked Makheneb. "This isn't like your home."

"No," said Makheneb. "More trees at home, and a lot less water."

At the gate into the city proper, where people went in and out with their wares, there was an Elf waving people through
and now and then asking a question or looking at a slip of paper. They came up to him in a clear little caravan, walking
beside their horses, with Makheneb in front. The Elf was finely dressed, with beautiful hair, and to Lily's surprise he
smelled very nice.

"Friend from Mirkwood," he said. "Who are all these with you? I am Edrain, the steward."

"It's good to make your acquaintance. We have been long on the road," said Makheneb.

"Do you have a host?"

"Not yet." They had met some of the Fair Folk on the road coming this way, Celenneth and Linnea and Eryndil, and had said
they might meet them here. Edrain did not know them.

"Well," he said, "in the absence of a host, and that can change, of course, you are welcome at the Dwarven House. We have
room for any guest." It was just outside Nevlond's main gate, a little inward and to the left. One of the Elves at the gate
would take them. There were Fair Folk available if they had business in the city and wished to arrange it. "We only ask that,
if you have no host, you go with an escort. With an escort, the city is yours to explore and to do your business in. We are
glad to have you."

"Heading west?" he asked Makheneb. "Over the sea?"

"No. Not yet. It has not come yet, for me. Perhaps someday, if I am invited."

Edrain seemed to assume that of course Makheneb would go; why would anyone not? But he respected the decision to wait.

## The Dwarven House

The Dwarven House filled a whole city block, one single long building, with beautiful balconies that ran all the way around
the upper floor. Each of them was given a room. It was not an inn, where you pay and are nobody in particular. Most visitors
here had a host, who paid their costs; this house was the catch-all for those who were not Elves and came uninvited, and it
was kept as a kindness. They would not pay for their rooms. Food would be brought. They might have to fit in with others'
times, and wait for an escort to be free if they wanted to go somewhere.

To Perry, the escort seemed at first a question: *do you not trust us?* Then he thought about it as a gatekeeper would. There
were people who ought to be escorted in Bree and were not. He understood it completely. He would rather somebody told him
when he was doing something wrong, since he was bound to, and he did not want to be arrested at the water's edge.

The rooms were clean, and smelled of the sea. Before dinner they stood on the balcony and watched the sun go down over the
water. The city went on across the firth, down to the water on one side and on the other rising sheer on bluffs hundreds of
feet high, and a mile and a half on there was simply a beach. Perry could not take his eyes off the sea. He was afraid of it,
a little, the way someone who fears heights is afraid of a great height, and it was the most beautiful and dizzying thing he
had ever seen. The world was very large.

He put on an Elvish robe, and as he did he felt the honeycomb in the pocket of his trousers, and remembered it was meant for
Círdan. They would meet him at some point. He pushed the trousers, honeycomb and all, under the bed, so that no one would come
in and throw them away.

Makheneb set about cleaning himself up. Elves do not shave, but he had been on the road a long time, and Edrain's standard of
dress was a good deal finer than most. Makheneb's own had been a poor place to start. He meant to make himself passable
before he went among his kin. He had money now, and could buy things without an escort. He meant to find a weapon-master, an
Elf if he could, and perhaps someone who knew Númenórean work, and a tailor, and perhaps new leather armour. If they were going
to stay a while, and they had healing to do, it would be a couple of weeks at least.

Marigold took out her journal and began to draw what she had seen.
