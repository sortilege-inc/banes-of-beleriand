## The mushroom-gatherer

Marigold Cotton had crossed the Brandywine for mushrooms. Her children were grown and settled about the
Shire, her husband was dead, and she had taken to going further afield than a respectable hobbit
ought. Even so, the river had always been a boundary to her. She had never crossed it before.

The Old Forest smelled different from anything on the Shire side. A garden smells one way and a
common another, and the woods of the Shire different again; but this was older than any
of them, a thick musky smell of loam and leaf-rot and wet bark. She found a few mushrooms quickly,
and then more, and many were kinds she had never seen. She kept a sense of the way back. She also
went in deeper than she had meant to.

Then she heard singing. It was a man's voice, a deep baritone, loud without bellowing, and it filled
the trees. It came from a brook nearby, and the song seemed to be about fish.

Marigold went towards it quietly, as only a hobbit can. The soil was soft under her bare feet. At the brook stood a big man — big even for one of the Big Folk, some six feet and more, and
slim — skipping stones down the water. He was very good at it. The stones went in long low arcs and
bounced four and five times. She watched until he threw a particularly fine one, and then she
could not help herself.

He turned at once and swept her a grand bow. "I didn't know I had an audience! Come and try your
hand."

She ran down and tried her hand. She kept her apron gathered up, with the morels hidden in it, but
he sniffed the air hugely and smiled as if he knew exactly what was there.

"The south side of the oaks is where the best mushrooms are," he said. "Goldberry is making some
for supper tonight. Will you join us? The name's Tom. What's yours?"

"Marigold."

"Marigold! Wonderful."

He led her through the wood, singing again. This song was in a language she did not know. It was
beautiful, and a little sad in a way she could not place. They came to a small cottage with
animals wandering about it, and Tom burst in at the door.

"Goldberry! I've brought a guest for supper!"

A fair-haired woman stood over a great pot on the fire. She welcomed Marigold as though she had
been expected, and there were bowls already poured.

## The Elf and the bear

Makheneb was in the Old Forest too, for his own reasons.

He was an Elf of Mirkwood, a Warden of that wood, young for his people at something under three
hundred years. He had fought in the Battle of Five Armies. For the last while his luck had been
bad; his family had fallen on hard times long ago, and lately he had lost nearly everything he
carried, down to the shirt on his back. He had come west on foot and lived off the land.

What drew him was a feeling. Once or twice in his long life he had been near something truly evil
— near wraiths, or things like them — and each time there had been the same
prickle down the spine, the same awareness of a presence close by. What he felt now was not that.
It lacked that depth of malice. But it was alien, and it had a pull to it, and for some time it had
drawn him westward without telling him why.

He cast about the forest for its source and found nothing he could follow. But he knew where he
was. He had heard his elders speak of this country as Tom's land, and they spoke of Tom as older
than themselves, and not an Elf, and that had always puzzled him. They did not speak of Tom with
awe. They mentioned him in passing, the way one mentions the weather: *and then the thing went off
into Tom's land*, and that was the end of the story.

As the light began to fail he heard a woman singing, far off, and a man's voice joined hers. He
stood listening. Behind him something snuffled.

It was a bear, something like a black bear, and much too close. Makheneb held his bow ready and
made no threatening move, and began to back away slowly towards the singing. The bear came on. He
did not trust his feet in the gloom, and the first blow nearly caught him. He began to run between
the trees, weaving from trunk to trunk to keep them between himself and the bear, and blows that
would have taken his face off shuddered into bark instead.

Then the song he had been following broke off. A little later a new one began, closer: a low
voice, singing a slow and soothing song in a language Makheneb did not know. The bear pulled up.
It sat back on its haunches with its forepaws in front of it and stared at him sullenly.

The song had a refrain that came round and round. Makheneb caught it and began to sing it too. The
bear did not seem to mind.

Out of the dusk came a tall man in bright yellow boots. He laughed, scratched the bear behind the
ears, and rubbed its head. "Be off with you," he said, and the bear stumbled away into the trees.
Then he looked Makheneb up and down.

"An Elf! Welcome, welcome to Tom's land! I'm Tom."

"I have heard much and little of you," said Makheneb.

"Well then, come and have supper."

"I have not had a good meal in quite some time."

## The runaway pony

Lily Goatleaf had left Bree in a hurry. She waited tables at the Prancing Pony, and over the years
she had listened closely to the Rangers when they trusted someone enough to talk. She had
learned from them that Bree-land was not the safe place Bree-folk took it for. It was a small
island with enemies all round it that no one else could see. She had never told anyone. They would
not have believed her.

Lately the folk coming through Bree had grown stranger and more unsavoury, and they were gathering
there. Then a Ranger called Findemir passed through the inn. She overheard him, speaking low, and
learned that he was setting out west; and he was gone again before she could speak with him. She
felt a dire portent in it, and decided she could not wait any longer. She borrowed a half-starved pony called
Buddy on the vague promise that she needed to leave town for a while, and went after the Ranger to
bring back help.

Perry Oakroot saw her go and came with her: not to bring her back, but to help. They were distant cousins, of a kind that depends on which way you
count. Perry was a Man of Bree and one of the gate-wardens of Bree-land, and he had spent many cold
lonely nights on watch at the gates with only the stars and the owls for company. He knew a good
many people in town, and a good many travellers by sight. He had come to know some of them as good
folk, not at all like Bill Ferny and his cronies. He had not been free from fear or care for some
time.

They were a day out of Bree on the East Road, walking and leading Buddy with their gear on his
back. Lily hoped Findemir had at least stopped in Hobbiton, and that someone at an inn there
could tell them where he had gone. Dusk was coming on and they were talking about where to camp
when something frightened Buddy. He bolted off the road and down into the forest, and everything
they owned went with him.

Lily sprinted after him at once. Perry stood in the road for a moment. He was thinking of Butterbur,
and what Butterbur would say. Then he ran after her. The ground fell away steeply from the road. Lily was quick
and sure-footed, and she overtook the pony a little way into the trees and began to calm him.
Perry came down the slope with more speed than he had meant to. At the first stand of trees a root
caught his foot and he fell flat on his face.

He got up at once, took his bearings, and went on at a measured jog, watching the ground for roots
the whole way.

While Lily soothed Buddy, another horse came walking towards them out of the trees. It had no
tether and it was plainly well cared for, and it came up to them without any fear at all. It
seemed to be hoping for a treat. Lily edged towards it carefully, as one approaches a new friend,
and it let her.

Perry stumbled out of the wood covered in twigs, his face smeared with dirt. "What happened, Lily?"

Lily was already rummaging in the pack for an apple. She gave Buddy one and offered another to the
strange horse, but it turned and cantered off.

"We should be careful," said Perry. "There are strange folk in these parts."

"Strange folk can't have horses?"

"We should follow it. It may even be the Ranger's. But carefully."

They followed. They passed through a garden, though it was not like any garden in Bree. Nothing was
fenced or set in rows. The vegetables tumbled over one another, and the beans zigzagged all the way
up to a cottage door. As they came round the corner of the house they saw a big man in bright
yellow boots and a blue jacket stroking the horse's mane and feeding it something.

Lily called out to him. The man turned to the horse. "Strawberry, you've brought new friends! And
who's this?" He held out his hand, and there was an apple in it that had not been there before,
and Buddy took it.

"This is Buddy," said Lily. "I've only had him a day, and I would do anything for him."

"Anything for Buddy," said Perry. "What is your name, sir?"

"Tom. Tom, and yours?"

"Perry."

"Lily."

"Perry and Lily! Wonderful. Well, you must stay for supper. We have a mushroom stew, and the peaches
have come in, and there are wild strawberries not far off — hence the horse. I go and gather them
now and then." He promised that Buddy would have plenty to eat. Lily told him they had been trying
to fatten Buddy up the whole way. It had been their pet project.

## Supper

Tom brought them in and introduced his wife, Goldberry, and she sat them down beside Marigold.
Perry had seen hobbits at the Prancing Pony, which kept rooms for them, and dealt with a few at the
gate, but he could count on one hand the hobbits he had ever spoken to. Lily introduced herself.
Marigold was glad to see young people, and before long she had brought out her mushrooms and was
teaching Lily their names. Lily listened. She liked learning things. But the Ranger was never far
from her mind.

Tom and Goldberry were perfect hosts, and nothing seemed to trouble them. Their land was bountiful,
but they did not seem to work it hard or to fret over it. Animals wandered in and out of the house
as they pleased. Nothing was penned and nothing was tied. Strawberry ambled where she liked. There
was a pumpkin patch in one corner that made no sense where it was.

After supper Tom asked, "You don't mind if we sing a little, do you?"

Lily said she would love it. They built up the fire and served tea and small cakes, and Tom and
Goldberry took turns singing. Some of the songs were in languages the guests knew and some were
not. The words they understood were mostly tender things about the land: a walk on a warm spring
day in the rain, and how the rain felt; the moment when the sun catches a stream just so. But now
and then a verse spoke of the land in ways that did not match anything they knew of it, or named
people and places none of them had ever heard of.

In the middle of a duet Tom stopped.

"Sorry, my love. The bears are getting a little anxious. I'll be right back." And he walked out of
the door.

Perry asked whether there was danger, and whether Tom might want help.

"We don't usually have trouble with the bears," said Goldberry. "But sometimes travellers meet
them, and then there can be trouble. Tom likes to make sure nobody gets too ornery. I wouldn't fancy
your chances if you came to fisticuffs with a bear." Then she said, "I expect we'll have another
guest. I'll warm up some supper. Would one of you set a place, and fetch some wine?"

They set a place. Before long Tom came back with Makheneb behind him.

"I knew to expect somebody," Tom said. "I must admit I wasn't quite expecting you lot. We'll talk
once you've finished. Eat! Sit! Enjoy yourselves!"

Makheneb ate like one who had not eaten for some time. Now and then he glanced round the room. Tom
settled by the fire with a pipe whose bowl was nearly the size of a small dish, and offered a pipe
to anyone who wanted one. His mood had grown quieter. Goldberry got out her knitting, put it away
again, and went about tidying.

"There are not many of your people outside the Shire," Makheneb said to Marigold.

"You'd be surprised how many hobbits love to see the world," said Marigold. "Nothing surprises me
out here." She had never seen an Elf before. On the inside she was wondering what to do, and whether
she had already offended him.

He had not seen many hobbits either. There were few in Mirkwood; one, in living memory. But he had
been at the Battle of Five Armies, and he knew the tale of the hobbit who had smuggled the Dwarves
out of the Elvenking's halls.

"What brings two Men of Bree out here?" he asked.

"Is it so plain that we're from Bree?"

"I can smell it."

"Chasing a pony," said Perry. "And what brings an Elf of Mirkwood this far west?"

"A feeling," said Makheneb. "Not good, and not bad. Strange."

"The Shadow grows in the East," said Marigold.

"And he's come west," said Perry.

"Perhaps the Shadow has," said Makheneb.

Perry said they had felt something too. Unsavoury folk were gathering in Bree, and Bree needed
help. Lily turned to Tom and Goldberry and described the Ranger they were chasing. She said he was
tall, by which she meant anyone taller than a Bree-lander.

Goldberry knew him at once. "Oh — Findemir! He came through only the other day." She described him
better than Lily had: a bushy, curly auburn beard, no moustache, and hair that curled too, though
not tightly.

"That's why you're here," said Tom. "That makes perfect sense. That is why you're here."

Then he said, "You aren't wrong to feel there are ill winds about. It seems things have been loosed
that need to be put back to sleep. Findemir was going north, beyond Fornost, to Barad Tarminalë. He is looking for
help. The Rangers are dealing with strange creatures coming through the woods, and he believes some
old power has loosed them. They have started coming into the Old Forest. I've had to kick one or two
of them in the hind parts."

"What do they look like?"

"The one I met had the form of a wolf. It was a good deal bigger than most wolves, and it was
alone, which is odd in a wolf. Its fur was white and greyish. And though we are having a lovely
spring here, it carried the hoar-frost of winter with it."

"So the one we're looking for is looking for help with strange creatures," said Marigold. "And we
are that help?"

"Within the Old Forest my power is complete," said Tom. "Beyond it, I will give whatever aid I can.
You have my blessing and my support in all you do, and I would dearly like you to come back and tell
us what Findemir has found. You have a hobbit in Marigold, an Elf in Makheneb, and two good Men of
Bree. I believe that with you we will get to the bottom of this, and set the wild things right
again."

Makheneb had another realisation, once the bear had worn off and his belly was full. This house was
the heart of it. The strangeness he had followed west came from here. He had
travelled west to find it, and now that he had, he was not glad. He had already been robbed down to
his shirt, and the journey had hardly begun. At least, he thought, it had not all been hunger.

So the four of them became a company, with Tom Bombadil and Goldberry as their patrons, and Tom's
house as a refuge to return to.

## Freeze-berries

The guests slept on bedrolls and cots that night. Marigold and Lily were up before dawn, both of
them early risers, and they woke to the smell of something like strawberry muffins. Tom was in the
kitchen with flour to the elbows and flour on every surface, pulling a tray out of the oven.

"Eat them while they're hot!"

There were strawberries in them, and another berry none of them knew, with a tartness like
rhubarb. "Freeze-berries," Tom said. "They only grow in the Old Forest. They come up just after the
last snow is gone, and they're only about for a few weeks."

Goldberry came in an hour later from seeing to the animals, and by then Perry and Makheneb were up.

Over breakfast somebody asked Lily, quietly, "Is he a Man?"

"I don't think so," said Lily. "But if you asked me anything at all this morning, I'd say I don't
think so."

They turned to Makheneb, who as an Elf was presumed to know. "He is not of my kind. Even the
eldest of my kin know little of him. I half thought he was a tale. But here he is. He seems a good
one to have owe us a favour."

"The man, the myth, the legend," said Tom, coming in at the door. He had plainly heard every word.

They spread Perry's maps on the table, weighing the corners down with muffins. The maps had come
down to him from his grandfather Herbert. Perry asked Tom which way he would go: east along the Road
and then north to Fornost, which might be quicker, or straight north across country if speed
mattered. Tom said roads were kinder to ponies. Then he ran a jammy finger over the map and stopped. "Oh, that won't do. That
hasn't been there for a couple of generations." He rummaged in his pockets for a stub of pencil and
began to mark the map in three places. He did not say what the marks meant.

There was a jam smudge on them now as well.

Goldberry led Strawberry over to Makheneb. "Your companions all have mounts. Your road will go more
smoothly if you have one too. We only ask that you bring Strawberry back to us within a year, when
you're done. Care for her as I would my own." Lily had Buddy. Perry had brought his old horse, Leaper, who
had earned the name long ago and no longer deserved it. Marigold had a pony too, Margarita. All of
them were a little jealous of Makheneb.

Goldberry gave them a basket of the mushrooms left over from supper, some of them kinds even
Marigold had not seen, and a few jars of preserves. They set out for Barad Tarminalë, ten days'
journey away, in the spring of the year 2965.

## The road north

They kept to the road. Not far along it, riding in the sun on a fine spring day, they saw the bear.

It had seen them too, and it was ambling towards the road on a line that would meet them. Makheneb
began to sing the song Tom had sung to it. He got one verse out before he told the others to join
in, and they did, though none of them knew what the words meant:

> *Bârun, Bârun, zîrân lô,*  
> *Uzûn bâri nardim tô.*  
> *Katha lô îdô kalab,*  
> *Nardê batîr, nardê khâlab.*  
> *Zimra-nîdô, azra-lad,*  
> *Aphadad, lô nardad!*  
> *Nimmê, Bârun, târik bal,*  
> *Anadê thâni, nardê zal.* The bear came up to the road and fell in
beside Strawberry, and walked along with them for two hours. Then it seemed to grow bored and
wandered off another way.

When the song died down and they had wet their throats, the questions came. "Are you friends?"

"It is an Elf thing," said Makheneb.

"Now you know what to do if you meet a bear," said Makheneb. "I would advise exactly that."

One of them tried the song alone a little later. It did not go well.

"Maybe it only works when we sing it together," said another. "I think we could make a drinking
song of it."

They passed west of Bree at the crossroads and went north up the old road without going into the
town. Perry was relieved. He had not asked for much leave from the gate, and he did not care to explain
the whole adventure to everyone in town. Thanks to Tom and Goldberry they had all the
food they needed.

Three days or so out, the road began to change. The roads east and west and south of Bree were kept
up well enough, but the North had not been a place of trade for a long time, and on this road they
met no one at all. Lily scouted ahead, riding forward and falling back to rejoin the others. On one
of her rides she came to a place where the road had been swept away. For a few hundred feet it was
simply gone: shoved up, broken, and pushed under the earth.

Her first feeling was awe at whatever had had the strength to do that. Her second was dread,
because it did not look like the work of wind or flood. It looked as if someone had done it. They
picked their way round it with care, and it wearied them.

After that the going was better. They found a path through the foothills that no one seemed to
use any longer. It had been well trodden once, and no trees had grown up in it. They followed it and
made surprisingly good time.

## The woman in the foothills

In the foothills Makheneb went off from the path, drawn by some smell or some sense of wrongness,
and found the dead.

There were four of them, mostly bone, and long dead. Three were Orcs or goblins of some kind. The
fourth had been an explorer, well equipped for the Wild and nothing like a travelling merchant. Her
body was much of a size with his own, and her helm and shield were still sound. He had gone
without both since his luck turned, and he took them.

Makheneb did not want to leave them lying as they were. There were trees enough there, though it
was not quite a forest. He gathered what personal things he could find, and then he laid the dead
to rest in the way of his people. The earth and the growing things closed about them faster than
any earth should, and the green grew over them. He said a few halting words for them. He
wished them rest and asked that their fathers might look on them. He felt sure afterwards that no
wight or wraith would rise from that place.

While he worked, he saw clearly how she had died. She had been caught off her guard by three or
four Orc-warriors. She had killed two of them and maimed a third so badly that he could not move,
and the others had left him there. Then they had killed her.

Beyond the foothills Perry's maps ran out. None of them had been this far north, and none of them
knew the country. Lily was scouting again, and she led them wrong for half a day. The broken road
still troubled her. She had been swept up in the adventure — in Tom, and Strawberry, and the new
friends — and the broken road had brought her back to why she had set out. It disheartened her, too:
she had only just left Bree, and already she was seeing more of the thing she had left to find help
against.

A day and a half later they saw the shape of hills that Tom had described. Coming in from the west
by the wrong road turned out to be lucky. The fort was a ring of stone, nearly cut into the hills,
and it lay mostly on their northern and western faces. From the south, the way they should have
come, they might never have seen it.

## Barad Tarminalë

Lily knew more than the others about the Dúnedain. The Rangers had often talked more freely with her
than with most folk in Bree, because she was gently curious and polite, never prying, and she had
the innkeeper's habit of hospitality. They had told her of their own customs of hospitality. They
kept way-stations across the wild lands. There was nearly always a steward at such a place,
taking turns with others, to keep a fire lit and the house open. You could turn up unannounced,
even after a month in the wild, and find the plainest of comforts, and help if you were badly
hurt. She guessed this was such a place. She could not be sure the welcome would extend to people
who were not Rangers, but she thought the odds were good.

"We should go up openly," she said. "As what we are: tired folk looking for someone. Nothing to
make them wary. They'll give us the plain welcome, but they are Rangers, and they're suspicious by
nature."

They agreed to go up to the gate together and speak from a little way off, so no one inside would
think they were rushing it. There was an hour and a half of daylight left. The sun sat just above
the hills.

The gates were great stakes of timber that opened inwards. There was no one on them. Lily went a
little ahead of the others. "Hello?" No answer came. She called louder. "I'm looking for Findemir!"

"One minute!" a woman's voice called from within.

The gates creaked. A woman had hold of a big iron ring on one side and was walking it slowly
backward. It was a heavy gate, but not a hard one once it was moving. "Come in, come in," she said.
Then she saw the rest of them and looked startled. "Well! What a party. Come in and warm
yourselves."

"What's your name?" asked Perry.

"I'm Moriel."

"Moriel. I'm Perry, and I'm pleased to make your acquaintance."

She was a sturdy woman, dressed as a Ranger, in good boots, with one of the heavy cloaks they
wear against the rain. It was a little much for the season, though the nights were still cold. She
looked as if she meant to be on the road before long.

"You've only just missed Findemir," she said. "But I can help you a little. Come and eat something,
and let me get you settled." She shut the gate behind them and showed them round.

Inside, the place was all put away. There were cabins with bare beds, and cupboards that were
mostly empty. Moriel kept a small store in one of them, and she began taking out vegetables for a
stew. She had eaten already, she said, but they could cook something together. She talked freely,
and she was curious about everything: how they knew Findemir, and what had brought them all the way
up here.

They told her plainly that they were looking for Findemir. Bree needed help, he had seemed a capable
man when he came through, and he had said he might need help himself in the north. They did not
mention Tom.

She was the only one there. She was waiting for someone and would go west in a day or two, when they
arrived. She had kept the winter at this post for some months, and only three travellers had come
all season. It was her first winter here. At the places she had wintered before there had been
thirty or forty people, all hunkered down for the cold, and it had been more of a festival. Here
there had been a month and a half with no one at all. She had invented a good many games in that
time and got very good at beating herself, but she could not promise she would be much of an
opponent for anyone else. Lily offered to teach her a card game she knew, for two or three or four
players, and Moriel was glad to learn it.

Findemir had been there only a night, more or less. There was a task of caretaking that had to be
done every year, Moriel said, and this year it was his turn. It lay a day and a half's journey
north-east. She had never gone herself, but she knew others had, and she told them what she knew.

Perry and Lily had heard the word *Númenórean*. Now and then a Ranger would turn up in Bree with some
old thing: a piece of pottery, a tool, a dagger. The designs were strange, and the things were
sometimes said to have strange properties, and the Rangers always said the same thing about them:
that no one knew any longer how such things were made. They called them Númenórean. Lily and Perry
had never been sure what that meant — only that there had once been a great people in the North, a
very long time ago.

Findemir's errand was to a place like that. There was a ruin of some kind, and it had to be tended,
like grounds that must be kept. That was where he had gone.
