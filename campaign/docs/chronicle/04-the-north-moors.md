## Fellow travellers

For some days the Company travelled west with Celenneth and her household: Linnea, her partner;
their two children; and the Elf Eryndil, who rode a horse laden with heavy bags. They skirted the
southern end of the great lake of Evendim, and the ruins of Annúminas lay ahead of them.

Celenneth's party was bound for the Grey Havens. Lily had never heard of the Grey Havens. She had no
idea how far west it was, only that it was west. To her the name sounded like a refuge, a place of
shelter, the sort of place where people went when they had nothing. That puzzled her, because
Eryndil was plainly a person of some standing, with fine clothes and a very fine horse, and did not
look poor or hungry in the least. But you could not judge, Lily thought. You never knew what was
going on with people. One evening, when camp was made, Celenneth and Eryndil went off into the woods
for something and Linnea was busy with the children, and Lily took her chance. She stuffed their
packs with apples and whatever else she had that might spoil, and hoped it would all come in
useful. She did not ask anyone about it. She saw a problem, and she solved it.

Marigold asked Celenneth whether she had ever been in the North Moors. There were stories among
hobbits of tree-men there. Celenneth had travelled widely, but mostly by the roads. Once she had
crossed the hills of Evendim on her way to the mountains, and that had been much further north than
the Moors. She did not know this country. The only thing she had heard of it was a strange tree
that was said to stand there, called the Tree of Sorrow. She knew a little tale about it. The tree
had been made by many trees growing together, fusing themselves into one to shelter from some
threat. It was told like a fable, short and plain, and she could not vouch for any of it.

Már did not know the country either. Her people were of the Grey Mountains, far away in the
north-east, beyond Mirkwood; she had come the way they had all come, from the east.

## The scholar's library

Perry had a question for Eryndil. He had assumed that all Elves were immortal and had been about
since the dawn of time, if not before, so he asked her whether she had heard anything of what Tom
had called *Melkor's playthings*. She was a scholar, and she did not answer off the top of her head.
She waited until they had made camp.

Then she unloaded her horse. The heavy bags were full of books, one after another, and some were
great ancient tomes. She laid a piece of leather on the ground and stacked them on it with care, and
began to look through them for the thing she half remembered. Perry is a man who likes to handle
things. He tried to help, awkwardly, and flipped a little too hard through some of the oldest pages,
looking for pictures.

What she found was a verse from an old epic ballad, the kind of poem that gathers the great deeds of
the past into song. It spoke of beings none of the Company had heard of, called the Valar. They were
described almost as gods, though it was no religion the Company knew, and each had a domain of its
own. Aulë was the smith, and was said to have made the Dwarves; there were Ulmo, and Yavanna, and
others. The verse warned that Morgoth had made a weapon for each of the Valar, fitted to each one's
weakness, and that those weapons had been lost.

The Company told her what they had seen under the frosted floor. There was a creature like that in
the verse. It was named there as the weapon against Tulkas, the Vala of strength and of battle.

The language of the poem was overwrought and often vague. It had seven sections. Some of them were
cryptic. One spoke of *a collection of the corrupted souls of the Immortal*. One named something
called *Maiar*. And one was called *Lúthien's Disguise*.

Eryndil was very careful about all of it. The ballad moved the way such poems do, she said. It
zoomed in on a single deed, done in a single year by a single person, and then it zoomed out into
broad mythic language, and in those passages you could not tell within a few hundred years when it
was speaking of. At best you could say the beginning, the middle or the end of an age. Elsewhere it
named mountain ranges and cities, but often by names they no longer bore. She told them what she
thought the verse meant, and then told them not to quote her on it.

The floodgates had opened for Perry some time ago. He had always known there were big things out in
the world beyond Bree, and now he was learning of so many at once that none of it could shock him
any longer. There were evil things, and dead gods, and Immortals going about. Of course there were.
He trusted all Elves implicitly. Anything, just then, seemed possible to him.

"Did you ever meet Melkor?" he asked.

"Thankfully, no," said Eryndil. "I came of age in the days of Arnor."

"We just passed Fornost," said Perry. "What was Fornost like?"

Fornost, and Annúminas ahead of them, had both been remarkable, she said. This had been the kingdom
of Men for many, many years. There had been a time of great battles in the east of Eriador, when
evil came down out of Carn Dûm, and the battles ran along Fornost and all the way south towards
Tharbad. The south-west had been spared, but much of the land about them had been fought over, and
some of it overrun, and the kingdom had fallen. Gondor had lasted far longer, in the south.

And west of here, she said, was the sea: the greatest of all seas. There were whole lands in it,
islands as large as all this country, and more than once such lands had been sunk beneath the waves.

Perry had not known there was a sea. He pictured an island in the middle of a lake, not a very big
one, and could not see how anyone could sink it.

Across the ocean, Eryndil told him, lay another land entirely. Sadly, the children of Men were not
permitted to go there.

"I've only just found out about something," said Perry, "and I can't go there? I want to see the
sea. Why can't we go?"

Eryndil looked at him. She said it was reserved for those who do not die of old age.

Perry saw at once that she was lying to him. She had been so precise about everything else, and now,
all of a sudden, she was vague and opaque, like someone softening a thing she knew and did not want
to say. He pressed her. Could he not simply take a boat?

"Your ancestors did not come in peace," she said. They had tried to take what was not given, long
ago, and so the way west was barred to Men.

"My grandfather? These are his maps."

"Perhaps your grandfather's grandfather's grandfather's grandfather."

So passage was not permitted to Men. Perry took it hard. He had only just learned there was such a
thing as the Undying Lands.

Eryndil looked at him with some interest. "I'll be honest," she said. "I don't know that anyone has
ever tried."

"There'll be one," said Perry.

## Tom's names

Lily had a question too. When the Company had told their story, had Eryndil ever heard of Tom and
Lady Goldberry?

Eryndil had. "Iarwain Ben-adar," she said. That was Tom's name among her people, and it meant *the
oldest, and fatherless*. "Tom was in these lands when my people first came to Eriador. All these
lands were Tom's lands." What were counted his lands now, she could not say. "You would have to ask
Tom. I have heard nothing but good of him in the songs. He is only strange."

Eryndil had children of her own. The youngest was about three hundred and fifty. One had already
sailed west across the ocean. The other was dead, and had died at the Battle of Five Armies.

Már had gone out to that battle too, though not in the front line; she was too needed at home, where
things had to be kept running. She had gone out to meet the host when she was wanted, and they wanted
her now and then, for the dead.

## The bow

Before the two companies parted, Makheneb showed the others the great bow he had taken from the
ruins. Something had been wrong with it all along. In the ruins every sense he had was flooded, the
way it is hard to pick out one noise in a loud room. Now that they were far away and his senses had
settled, the wrongness remained, and he knew it was the bow itself.

Eryndil and Celenneth looked it over with him. It was Númenórean, clearly. It might even be the Great
Bow of Angnir. There was a shadow upon it. But it had also been blessed with a straight flight, and
an arrow loosed from it would not be turned aside.

The others were told about the ballad, and the Valar, and the corrupted souls of the Immortal.
Makheneb, at least, had heard of the Valar before, in the tales of the making of the world. Morgoth
had been one of them once: the note of discord in the great song, who turned against creation and
tried to make things of his own. He had also heard it said that Orcs were Elves, once, corrupted.

## Parting

Perry's travelling went well, and they were making good time. A little past the lake, in the
morning, the two companies parted. Celenneth's household went on west to meet the coast and follow
the river down to the Grey Havens.

There were islands in the lake that might be explored. But they had just heard what happened to
islands, and they were not sure islands were safe any more. Instead they turned south towards the
Shire, meaning to pass through the North Moors on the way and look for the Tree of Sorrow.

Marigold had been born a North-Took, before she married a Cotton. She had distant relations up here
whom she had met once or twice, at family gatherings, though the bulk of the family lived nearer
Hobbiton and east of it.

In the wild country Makheneb took his rope and grapnel and climbed one of the tallest trees to look
around. The tree was not far off, perhaps three or four hours to the west. It was a very strange
tree. It looked knotted, as though several kinds of trees were twined together, and it stood half
again as tall as anything around it.

The forest here was feral. It was foothill country, much wilder than the woods near Bree, like the
Chetwood, that have travellers passing through them all the time. As they came nearer, the trees
began to thin, and through gaps in the leaves they caught glimpses of the great tree high above them,
and steered by it.

## The standing stones

It was well past midday when they came out on a small hill crowned with six standing stones, each
eight or ten feet tall. They tied the horses there. Makheneb had seen such places before; this was a
good spot for watching the stars once night fell, and for any work to do with them. That was
probably what it had been built for, and perhaps for other rites besides.

The stones were rough-hewn, but they had been quarried and stood up on purpose. One of them was new.
There were grooves in the hillside where a sledge had dragged it into place, overgrown now, but not
old. This was a place that someone still tended. It was no forgotten ruin.

Beyond the hill stood the tree. It was a mass of great roots rising out of the ground, and above the
roots a trunk that looked like forty trees congealed together. Már saw that there were gaps among the
roots, and some looked more deliberate than others. There might be a path in. Lily, squinting up
into the branches, thought she saw something like a treehouse, some two hundred feet up, though
there was no sign of anyone about.

Perry's first thought was Elves.

"I don't think most of us live in trees," said Makheneb. "Most of us in Mirkwood live in caves,
strange as that may be."

Lily admitted then, a little late, that she had assumed all Elves were as poor as Makheneb, and had
secretly stuffed Eryndil's packs with food. Perry, for his part, was still cross about his
grandfather's grandfather's grandfather's grandfather.

Whoever lived here might not want visitors, and whether they went up to the treehouse or into the
roots, they would be intruding. So they decided to announce themselves. They would do it with a
song. The one they sang best was the one they had learned from Tom, the song for bears, and they
sang it with a will. Perry had a cajón and beat time on it. What he lacked in voice he made up in
enthusiasm, and Marigold's voice carried them. At worst, they said, whoever heard it would only think
there was a bear in the woods.

## The long-folk

When the song ended there was a sharp whistle far above them, four or five storeys up. Five or six
children were peering down from a limb of the tree, the smallest five or so and the eldest eleven or
twelve, and with them a grown woman with a bow. The bow was trained on the Company, but it was not
drawn.

The children had plainly enjoyed the song. They did not greet the Company; they only watched. The
eldest hollered something down in a language none of them knew. It sounded eager, and a little
curious. Then one of them tossed something down.

Már moved to catch it, but at the last instant she flinched. She thought it was something thrown at
her, and she stepped back out of its way. It struck the rock and shattered. As it broke she saw that
it had been a child's instrument, something like a pipe, thrown down to her as a gift. A cry of
distress came from above.

Már panicked. All she had to offer was the treasure she had brought out of the ruins. She held up one
of the silver goblets. The children were being steered away by then, and there was a great deal of
talk among them. The woman had still not drawn her bow. "Maybe they should teach their children not
to throw things," Már muttered.

The people above were nearer to Men than to Elves or Dwarves, but they were stretched: long in the
limb, long in the neck, lanky in every part.

The Company laid their weapons down in the circle of the standing stones and walked forward with
their hands open. Lily went first. She smiled, without showing her teeth, and made signs, and at last
said aloud, "I don't speak that language. This is what I speak. Do you understand?"

The woman lowered her bow and gave a very sharp whistle through her fingers. From the path among the
roots came three young men, perhaps six feet and a half tall, lanky like the rest, and armed. They came
up slowly, watching, and stopped at the edge of the circle. One of them turned to Lily and rattled off
a stream of thick speech full of *kh* and *th* sounds. It sounded rather like Dwarvish, though it had
nothing to do with Dwarvish at all. He looked at her as though expecting an answer.

Another of the young men went towards Makheneb's horse. The great bow stuck out of its case on
Strawberry's back, and it was huge. He made a grasping motion at it and then a throwing one, and spoke
urgently. Makheneb made it plain that it would be a bad idea to touch it. The young man seemed to agree
with him about something. Then he reached for the bow anyway and pulled it from its case.

Makheneb took it back out of his hands with an Elf's strength, as one takes something from a child.
The young man did not look angry. He looked intent and troubled. The second of the three came forward
and raised a dagger, and pointed, and his meaning was clear: *give it to him.*

Makheneb nocked an arrow and waited for them to make the first move. They were young, late in their
teens or not much older. He began to back away. "All right," he said. "It's probably time to go." The
Company still meant to leave in peace.

The goblet Már had offered lay where she had left it, beside the broken instrument. One of the young
men went over and picked it up and said something to the others, and made the same throwing gesture,
and put the goblet in a bag at his side.

Marigold thought it would look better if only one of them gathered up the weapons, rather than all of
them snatching up arms at once, so she took a bag and began to put the weapons into it. The young man
with the goblet nodded as though she was doing the right thing. When the bag was full he helped her
lift it, and the two of them carried it between them. Then he began to walk towards the roots, and to
tug her along with him.

Lily watched how they handled the weapons. They cared about them, but not as weapons; nobody was trying
to wield anything. It was the way you go through the house of someone old who has collected all kinds
of junk, and it is all going out on the rubbish heap. "I don't think they're robbing us," she said. "I
think they think they're doing us a favour, getting rid of these."

Perry could only think that they were being disarmed, and about to be sent back out into the wild with
nothing. They had met a bear on the road, and evil wolves in the hills. Without their weapons they would
not last.

Somebody looked up into the branches and counted at least three more archers up there, and another
figure near the way into the roots.

Marigold dug in her heels, and there was a small tug-of-war over the bag. He was much stronger than she
was, and she stumbled forward. Lily put on a great show of tears and heartbreak and desperation. The
young man holding the bag looked at her and said something to the other two, and they said something
sharp back, and there was a short hard exchange among them. Then he dropped the bag and spat on it.
The other two spat at the Company's feet and pointed away, and their meaning was plain: *get out, now.*

Lily nodded to them and thanked them, as though that were how people said goodbye here. Then the Company
took the bag and their horses and went, quickly, into the woods.

## What had happened

When they were far enough away to feel it was behind them, they tried to make sense of it.

They had wanted the Company to go in, somebody thought. They had wanted to be rid of the weapons.
Perhaps they thought the Company was a corrupting influence. They had gone straight for the bow; and the
bow, as far as anyone but Makheneb knew, was only a bow. None of them had ever heard of such people.
Marigold had had no idea that anyone like them lived so close to the Shire, and their language meant
nothing to her.

Should they sneak back, unarmed? Get a sample of their writing, if they had any, and show it to Tom? They
knew where these people were now. And the long-folk had never troubled the Shire.

Somebody tried to feel the place for any taint of Shadow. There was something there that was deeply
wrong. Perhaps the people were corrupted in some way, and their long bodies were a sign of it. Perhaps it
was nothing to do with them. The tree was strange and bizarre, but not necessarily evil. It was something
else, and not a quality of the people or of the tree. It was not necessarily evil. But it was wrong.

They marked the place on their maps. The Tree of Sorrow could wait. It was getting dark, and nobody
wanted to be there after dark. The Shire was only three or four hours away.

## Trappers

They headed south from the tree. Már was watching behind them in case the long-folk were following, and
because she was looking backward she did not see the snare until she had walked into it. It was set
already, a bent sapling and a noose, and she touched it and it went off. She caught it just in time and
threw herself clear. It gave her a great fright. It could have been the end of her. And where there was
one there might be more.

Voices came from up ahead. "I think we've got him, Fosco!"

Two hobbits came charging up and looked eagerly at the trap, and found it empty. They looked about in a
panic. One carried a stave upright and the other a small bow, and they saw Már.

"You've been stealing from our traps!"

"No, I have not been stealing from your traps," said Már. "This is the first one I've seen, and I only
just missed it. We'd like to avoid the rest of them, if you'd help us."

They were utterly unconvinced. "Do you know how much small game I've lost to your thieving?"

And Marigold knew them. The older was Fosco North-Took, a second cousin of hers once removed, and the
younger was his boy Rollo, who had been half his present height the last time she saw him.

"Marigold Cotton?"

"Oh, it's so nice to see you!"

"What are you doing, aiding and abetting this criminal?"

"Come on, let's go and eat!" said Marigold. "It's so nice to see you!" And she herded them all along
before anyone could think. The North-Tooks were deeply confused. A Dwarf had been robbing their traps,
and now here were Men and an Elf and Marigold coming out of the woods, and while they were still trying
to take it all in she had them walking home. "Well," said Fosco, "I suppose we can make you some tea."

## Long Cleeve

Long Cleeve is a little village on the edge of the Northfarthing, a handful of houses on either side of
the Water where it runs down out of the Moors. It is the home of the North-Tooks, a lesser branch of that
great clan, who claim to be descended from Bandobras Took, the Bullroarer himself. Their cousins further
south dispute it, but the dispute is kept friendly by shared family reunions, with drinking contests and
games of conkers and a golf tournament.

It was an hour or so to the house. A shallow stone wall ran round the house itself, though the land went
on well beyond it. There were neat gardens of flowers and vegetables, and wilder ones further out, and a
stack of chopped wood by a grove of trees, and chickens scratching in one corner.

On the way, the rest of the Company had been introduced, very briefly and not very well. Fosco repeated
their names back uncertainly. "Marigold, and, er, Perry, and Lilian." He scowled at the Dwarf and the
Elf. "And Mari. And Mackie."

"Peony!" he called out. "You'll never guess who I found at our traps!"

They had to duck to get in at the door. It was built to hobbit proportions, but there was room enough
once they sat down, and the Bree-folk were used to it; there are plenty of hobbits in Bree. After the
day they had had, it was a comfort to be in a house.

Peony's hair was more white than fair now, all bunched up on her head. She was delighted to see
Marigold, whom she had last seen at a family reunion some fifteen years ago, and there was panic in her
eyes too. "I'll be right back," she said, and bustled off to the pantry, and took Rollo with her.

Fosco looked round at them. "So what are you all, if you're not thieves?"

"We're friends," said Lily. "We do what Marigold tells us to."

"Well, if you're listening to Marigold, you can't be all bad."

"These are the friends who've been keeping me safe on my great adventures," said Marigold.

Fosco supposed that her adventures had taken her north into the Moors, and that was enough to prick his
interest. Then she told him just how far into the Moors they had been, further than he ever went
trapping, and that impressed him. Then she told him how far east she had gone, into the Old Forest, and
his eyes grew as big as saucers. "For mushrooms," she said.

Már was annoyed with him and held her tongue, and nodded along. Rollo came back and gave her a plate
first: thick slices of bread with butter and jam, and half a pear with the seeds cut out. "I'll be back
with yours in just a moment," he told the others.

Then Fosco turned to Már and asked what she had done. She had done nothing much in that battle with the
wolves, she said, but in the ruins there had been an undead thing, and she had been able to deal with it.
She told it plainly, with a little embellishment and a little suspense, but not boastfully: the dark
cave, the lanterns and torches they could not keep alight, the dead thing moving somewhere out in the
dark, and then her axe, and no more dead thing.

Fosco sat with his mouth open. He did not believe a word of it, but she told it so matter-of-factly that
his mind was racing. "So you mean," he said at last, "when they buried him, he wasn't actually gone?"

"Imagine you had a dead animal in your trap," said Már, "and the ghost of it sitting right next to it.
One is dead. The other is the undead part of it. That's what it was like. There was the man, clearly
dead, and his undead counterpart there too."

"Little old Marigold," said Fosco, "gone so far, and in such company."

## Peony

When the rest of them had been served, Peony sat down with them. She thought they were utterly full of
it, but she was far too polite to say so. She had married into the North-Tooks; Marigold had married out
of them. Peony thought, a little, that anyone who came up to the Northfarthing felt they had to brag and
bluster, and it was really not necessary.

She turned to Perry and Lily. "Are you from Bree? What brings you into the Shire?"

"We're accompanying Marigold on her travels," said Perry. "We're here to guard her while she goes about
finding mushroom patches. That's our main purpose. You could call it a quest."

"That's so sweet, that she's made friends with you."

Lily could no more watch someone play hostess without helping than she could fly. She went to find Peony
in the pantry, where she was cutting a loaf and handing things to Rollo to carry out. Peony pointed her to
the far end. "Grab a bottle of brandy, would you, and let's serve that up. And anything else that looks
good. You're so welcome."

While they worked, Lily chatted, and said that they had met Marigold and their paths had lined up for a
while. And Peony confided in her. "Fosco doesn't like to ask for help. But something keeps stealing the
game out of his traps. He loves trapping, and we haven't had a proper catch in six months. It's been
terrible. We've had to trade for everything." The garden did well enough, and they kept the rabbits out
of it; they were not destitute. "But if you could help, that would be wonderful. Only don't ask him.
Just say what you're going to do."

Perry came out carrying trays with far too much food on them, and set a great heap of nuts and dried fruit
in front of Makheneb. "Here, Mackie. Some extra birdseed." Then he turned to Fosco. "Speaking of that,
you said something's been stealing from your traps."

Rabbits, said Fosco. Small deer, even. He was a tracker as well as a trapper, and he thought it was a
person, not a hobbit. Perhaps a Dwarf, he said, and looked at Már. Sometimes it broke the traps, but most
of the time it left them fit to use again. It was nimble, then, and knew something about trapping.

Perry asked whether his neighbours had had any trouble. "Hamfast across the river says his traps are
fine," said Fosco. "But I think he's only saying it to upset me."

"How would we know your traps from Hamfast's?"

"His traps are shoddy. He likes snares that catch the leg. I like the ropes."

"We're going back up that way anyway," said Perry. "We'll let you know if we see anything."

They stayed the night. Peony took Fosco aside for a word, and it was settled. The North-Tooks had decided
that Perry and Lily were a couple, which they were not, and gave them Rollo's room. Lily left the bed to
Perry. There was a special mattress in a guest room for Marigold. Már was given a place in the stable,
where there were a couple of ponies and some pigs, and a ladder up to a loft full of hay; she took the
loft. Makheneb was offered the couch. Elves do sleep, whatever people think.

## Morning

Perry was up very early. He felt he owed the house something, so he went round doing the chores, tidying
the things they had left everywhere, and churning butter for hours. Rollo came out and found the day's
work already done.

"Did you tidy everything?"

Perry shrugged. "It's no trouble."

"That's normally my job. This is great." Rollo made breakfast: a heap of sausages and wonderful potatoes.
They raised their own pigs.

The Company met in the stable to plan. Somewhere in the North Moors was the place Tom had marked on the
map. Tom's mark was not exact. It was like saying that a thing lay where two rivers meet. The Tree of
Sorrow was in the general area of it, though that did not make the tree the place.

What would they even do when they found it? At the first ruin they had had Findemir's journal, a diagram
of how things ought to look. Here they had nothing. Even if they found the place, how would they know what
was broken? Perhaps the ring of standing stones was the first step. Perhaps it was nothing. They had begun,
among themselves, to call these places *banes*.

And could the long-folk be the ones stealing Fosco's game? Perhaps. But why would they start only six
months ago? They had surely been in that tree for a long time.

First, the traps. They would find one of Fosco's rope traps and bait it. Fosco sent Rollo to show them
where the traps were, and Rollo would stay with them for part of the day.

Már caught the rabbit. It is well known that Dwarves are natural sprinters, and she watched it and saw
where it was going and headed it off, and snatched it up. She had never killed a rabbit, and she did not
mean to; she wanted it to look as though the rope had caught it. So she bound it by the legs and hung it
in one of Fosco's rope traps, alive and struggling, where it could not get out. A live rabbit thrashing in
the leaves makes better bait than a dead one.

Marigold and Makheneb found hiding places near the trap. Lily and Már stayed further back with Rollo, and
Perry further still, with the horses; he knew his feet, and he did not trust them for this. Marigold was so
well hidden that Makheneb did not see her three feet away from him, and wondered where she had gone.

## The orc

They did not have long to wait. Something came out of the undergrowth.

Makheneb had seen Orcs before. He had fought them in battle. This was an Orc soldier. It wore one of their
conical helms with a bar down over the nose, and it carried a small spear on which it leaned as though
the spear bore some of its weight. It looked old, and frail, and it favoured one leg. It came up to the
trap and took hold of the rope with the rabbit on it.

In all Makheneb's experience, Orcs were the things he had fought in wars, or the servants of the
Necromancer in Dol Guldur. He knew of no other kind.

He was ready, and he loosed. The arrow struck true, and the Orc dropped where it stood with hardly a sound.
The rabbit went on bobbing up and down in its rope, quite unharmed. It happened so quickly and so quietly
that the others, chatting in low voices further off, knew nothing of it.

Marigold had seen it all. She and Makheneb waited and watched for a few minutes more, and listened. There
was nothing, no sound and no movement. It had been alone. Marigold poked it with a stick. It rolled over a
little, and they saw what it was wearing: a torn and battered cloak of the deep forest green of Mirkwood.
It had taken it from an Elf of the Woodland Realm. It might even have been one of the Orcs Makheneb had
fought against.

Makheneb gave the bird-whistle they all knew, and the others came up.

Rollo's eyes went wide. "What is that?"

"A creature of the Shadow," said Makheneb. "It has come a long way from the mountains to take your rabbits.
It looks frail enough. It must have been desperate. I've rarely seen them alone. Only when they've got
lost."

Rollo stood looking at everything, taking it all in. Where had the arrow gone? Into the spine. It was the
first dead body of anything but an animal he had ever seen, and it took some understanding.

While everyone else stood in shock, Lily quietly let the rabbit go.

Makheneb took care to remove the Mirkwood cloak from the body. The rest he left untouched.

For three of them, too, it was their first Orc. They had heard the stories, more of them than Rollo had,
but that did not make it less of a shock. The Shire was supposed to be the safest place in all
Middle-earth. Perry stood still for a long time. His work was to guard the gates of Bree, and this thing
must have passed that way. If one Orc could come so far west, past Bree and into the fields of the Shire,
how many more might? He felt that it was his fault, and that it was on him to do something about it.

"Who should know?" he asked quietly.

"Not everyone," said Marigold. "Not yet. But the Sheriffs, at least." Perry promised to carry word to the
Bounders and the Sheriffs.

It was getting late. Rollo looked to Marigold, and she sent him home. He went back through the dusk alone,
trying to make sense of what he had seen.

## Kingsworthy

The others followed the Orc's trail. Lily found it. She began where the rabbit had hung and watched the
brush, the bent grass, the scuffed moss. The Orc had been old and slow, and it had dragged one foot, and
once she saw the pattern of it the trail was as clear as day. It led east.

In half an hour they came to the Norbourn, hardly more than a stream at this point. Beyond it stood an old
stone tower: Kingsworthy, once a hunting lodge of the kings of the North. It was far older than any hobbit
settlement. Anything that had been made of wood, its doors and its shutters, had long since rotted away
or been carried off. The trail led straight into it.

It was dusk. There was no light in the tower and no sound. They watched it for an hour while it grew
quite dark, and saw nothing move. None of them knew whether Orcs were stronger in the dark. Makheneb had
hunted them by night in Mirkwood, and had never noticed.

They agreed that one of them would go in with a lantern where the rest could see the light, and the others
would wait outside.

The stench nearly knocked Lily backward. It was the smell of a barn that has not been mucked out in years:
filth on filth, and rot soaked into the stone. She put a cloth over her face and climbed the stairs. There
were bits of rotting offal from small animals, and bones, and dung. On the level above, something had been
living. In one corner were gathered little bits and pieces, sorted and kept: scraps of metal, a broken
sword, and a wooden shield of Dwarvish make leaning against the wall. None of it was worth anything. It had
only been kept. This had been the creature's nest, and it had lived here alone, for about the six months
Fosco had spoken of.

Lily shone her light from a window and gave the bird-call, and came back down. "It was alone," she said.
Makheneb was sure of it. The Orc had been an outcast, or a deserter, or perhaps an old soldier dying far
from whatever war it had come out of, living off the easy pickings at the edge of the settled lands.

The mood outside was heavy. For Lily and Perry and Marigold this was the first Orc they had ever seen, and
it was all wrong. The Shire, even the wild edges of the Moors, ought to be safe, and it was not.

## Back to Long Cleeve

It was late when they came back to Long Cleeve, and they did not wake the house. They made themselves beds
in the stable, Perry below and the rest up in the loft among the hay. No one said much. Sleep came slowly.

In the morning Peony and Rollo found them already at work, breakfast under way and the chores done. Peony
asked what had happened. Marigold told her gently that the thief had been found and dealt with. It had been
dangerous, yes. But it had been handled.

Perry apologised for what Rollo had had to see. It had been a monstrous soldier, he said, a very old one,
living out there alone. Perhaps it had been trying to retire. It was only the one, and it was dealt with,
and it would not happen again. If anything else turned up, they should let the Company know.

Fosco had warmed to them now that the trouble was over, though he could not quite bring himself to say so.
Rollo said it for him: he was really very sorry about earlier.

Then the Company talked about what came next. They had come to look into one of the places on Tom's map,
the rumoured site of a bane. It might be the Tree of Sorrow, and it might not, but it was near. After that,
they might well find they were out of their depth, and need to seek counsel from someone who knew far more
than they did. Tom was a great fellow, but he was no good at explaining things. The Grey Havens were not so
far off. There were the Dwarves in the Blue Mountains, too. Or they could go back to Bree and look for a
Ranger. Rivendell was too far.

For now they would stay close. They would leave the horses at Long Cleeve and go on foot, travelling light.
The Tree of Sorrow was half a day's journey north. They would go back to it once more, and try to understand
what waited beneath its roots.
