## Home

As they rode, Már told them what to expect. Dwarf-homes were tunnels, she said, and little rooms and
coves cut into the rock, and her family would take them in. Makheneb might get some looks; an Elf was
not a common guest under the mountains. And she reminded them, once more, never to speak Fenja's
other name.

Of her own people she spoke gladly. Her mother and father were there, and her sister Etta, who was
married to Onar. Etta and Onar grew things in the deep places of the mountain, food raised
without the sun, and Etta kept a bakery that made the most of it. They had twin boys, Már's
nephews. One of them wanted to follow his parents into the growing. The other, Nolly, had begun to
learn Már's own trade, the laying of ghosts, and his mother was not at all sure she liked it. His
grandparents were proud of him. There was a friend, too, Adis, who had hunted ghosts beside Már:
timid, but clever, the sort who would rather think a thing through than run at it.

Nenuviel knew nothing of mountains. She was going to judge the matter for Círdan, and she would lean
on the Company for the rest. Thramli's party, the Dwarves who had come to the Havens, had been five;
they had ridden home ahead of the Company. The broken Númenórean sword from the ruins had come
back from the smiths at the Havens reforged for Perry into a long-hafted axe, with a keen edge on it.

## The valley

They came down into the valley in the middle of a sunny afternoon, half a day from the nearest
settlement, and there they found Thramli's party.

All five were dead. Their bodies were scattered along the way, and they had not been killed by
beasts. It was the work of weapons. They had been dead less than two days; the scavengers were only
beginning.

Each of them took it differently. Makheneb went cold and still, and looked on as though from far
away. Perry went into the bushes and was sick. He had never seen anything like it. "This could have
been the road to Bree," he said, and could not stop saying things like it. Már's fear went straight
to her home. She thought it might be a punishment, for having promised help.

Dwarves care greatly for their dead, and Már knew what to look for. Rings, necklaces, sword hilts
and shields were heirlooms, and would tell which families these had been. One shield was very fine
and no heirloom at all; she marked it, for later. They loaded three of the bodies onto the horses and
went on foot beside them.

## The charge

Makheneb saw them first. Dwarves were coming at them, running, with hammers and axes raised. It was
no Dwarvish way of fighting. Dwarves were known for their shield walls, for close order and patient
defence; these came on wild, like beasts, and their eyes were rolled back white in their heads.

Among them Már knew her nephew. He was yelling, and she did not think he saw her.

"Már," said Makheneb, "should we shoot them?"

Már had no answer for that. She went forward instead and shouted to Nolly in Dwarvish, calling him by
his name, with the badger at her throat. He came to a sudden stop and stared at her, slack-jawed,
without understanding. Then slowly, as though she were coming into focus, he began to see her.

The others did not stop. Makheneb shot to stop them, not to kill. His first arrow struck one, and
slowed him, but the Dwarf did not act as a man acts who has been shot. The second went through
another's leg and pinned it to the ground, and the Dwarf pitched forward onto his own hammer. Perry,
who knew he was no archer, stood in front of Makheneb, and when a hammer came down he caught it on
the flat of his long-hafted axe. Marigold slipped away over the hill to hide. Lily held
the line with her sword.

Nenuviel fought from horseback with her spear, and she killed one of them, and did it gracefully.
Then a hammer crushed her leg and she fell from the saddle. The Dwarf she had speared through came
walking up the shaft, and wrenched the spear out of her hands.

A hammer took Már in the chest and broke her collarbone. She turned to a woman of her own people, one
with the white eyes, and tried to speak to her; no word of it went in. Afterwards she wished she had
not tried to reason with them at all.

Perry went in. He saw, on one of the Dwarves, a great axe-wound in the chest, fresh and open and not
healed at all, and a moment later he took an axe-blow of his own in the same place. He shrieked. When
it was over he had fallen down, spent, more hurt than he had ever been in his life.

Nolly let fall his weapon. "Már," he said. He knew her. He threw his arms around her and said he was
sorry, and sorry, and sorry again.

Marigold had come back, and an axe caught her, and she was bleeding. Már shook herself free of Nolly
and charged. Perry swung at the last of them still fighting, and missed; then all of
them together, Nolly too, wrestled him to the ground and bound him. His name was Veig. He was awake,
but he was not in his right mind.

There had been seven who attacked them. Three were dead. Two lay senseless, with Makheneb's arrows
in them, and Veig was bound. Nolly was himself again.

Nolly remembered nothing but being at home in the mountains. He did not understand why he was
outside, or why he had been attacking them, and he knew every one of the Dwarves who had just been
hurt and killed. He was distraught, and did whatever he was told.

## The last miles

They put Nenuviel on her own horse, with the bound Dwarf across it. The other five horses carried two
Dwarves each, the dead and the senseless, and everyone else walked. They would not come in until after nightfall.

Már urged them all to be ready to explain themselves, and she was counting on Nolly to bear witness.
Before they reached the settlement, Makheneb went among the bodies and drew out every arrow that was
plainly Elvish. Már walked beside the dead, setting them straight and making them presentable, over
and over. The last thing she wanted was for her people to take offence at some small thing. It all
looked well enough. She was nervous all the same.

Marigold and Perry could barely walk. They trailed behind with Nolly, trying to keep up with the
horses, and they went slowly, being friends to one another.

## The weigh station

It was a couple of hours after dark when they came to the first weigh station, and they were hailed.

Már gave her name, and her father's. "I have Nolly here with me," she said, "and we are trying to
travel home." They asked who all the rest were, and what their business was. "My companions," she
said, and named them: Marigold, Makheneb, Perry, Lily, and Nenuviel. They had been at the Grey
Havens, and spoken with Círdan, and they were making their way to the mountains. And then, because
there was no hiding it: they carried more than companions on their horses. They had been witness to a
tragedy, and been drawn into the fighting, and they had dead with them, and wounded, and they meant
to bring these Dwarves home to their families.

As the guards understood whom the Company was bringing, one of them broke into a wail. He stood tall
at his post while he did it, but the sound was piercing, bottomless, soul-crushing. The other took it
up too, and began to ring a bell in a set pattern.

Others came running down, and when they saw, they wailed as well. They took the reins of the horses.
They came alongside Perry and Marigold and Már and gave them an arm to lean on. Seven or eight of
them escorted the Company up into the mountain proper, and all the way they heard bells echoing
further up, and more and more people gathered along the way. Some were already looking at the dead,
and knowing them. It became a procession of grief, winding up the mountain and into the Hall.

## Elin

There one of the lords of the Dwarves met them. There was a council, and Elin sat on it. She was a
distant relation of Már's, the kind somebody had once mapped out for her, near enough that she knew
who Elin was and that they were kin. There was always one of the council awake, whatever the hour,
and tonight it was she. She came and welcomed them, and embraced Már.

"It is so good to see you," she said. "But what a moment of tragedy. Come. Come in." And then: "Are
there others?"

There were not. They had brought everyone.

Elin gathered people about her and began to give orders. The wounded went one way, for tending. The
dead went another, to be made ready. Then she asked what was to be done with the three who lived but
were bound.

Nobody had yet asked how they came to be dead, and Már did not tell it now. She spoke only of the
three. "We are concerned for their well-being," she said. When they had been awake their eyes had
been rolled back in their heads, and even Nolly had not known anyone for some minutes. There was no
telling how the others would be when they woke. "They may not know you," she said. She had been
lucky that Nolly did. Elin promised that they would never be left alone, and that they would be
relieved of their arms. Their weapons, she meant.

The best of the Dwarves' healers went to Nenuviel, who looked the worst, and they did much for her.
Marigold and Már were tended as well. Cots were put up and guest rooms made ready, with the promise
that there would be talk. "We will mourn the dead once they are prepared," they were told. "You
should rest. And then let us talk." Nothing seemed to be happening out in the valley that needed
anyone to go back that night. The talk would wait for morning.

## The first night

Már woke in a cold sweat from a dream she could not remember. It was still dark. The room was
something like a quiet house of healing, with a few others awake somewhere near, and she did not
want to disturb them. There were halls enough, and empty at that hour, and she paced them.

The Dwarves had been hospitable. But did they know? What was going through their minds? She turned
all of it over. There was something so deeply wrong in every part of it. It took her near an hour to
come to a place where she could bear it: that what had happened was not right, but she did not think
they had had a choice. There would be a way forward in the morning, she thought. With Elin, and with
Nenuviel, and with others, they might find an answer; perhaps Nolly would remember something. What had
happened had happened, and it was a tragedy. They had the means, at least, to find out what it was,
and to protect Nolly and the other Dwarves from it after. Let it never happen again. With that
resolve she went back to sleep.

Around Makheneb there were yards of stone, hundreds of them, maybe thousands, in every direction. He
was used to sleeping in caves, though not so deep as this. The rooms were centuries old, and nobody
worked the rock anywhere near the healing-rooms in the middle of the night. He found the cool
dampness of it comforting, and liked the quiet. Here he would hear anyone coming.

Marigold woke very early, and kept herself occupied until others were awake by drawing. She drew the
moment the Dwarf with the axe came at her, over and over, the same scene again and again.

## Morning

At about the sixth hour Már was woken by Etta, and found herself wrapped in a hug. When she had
pushed back enough not to smother, her sister stood away from her and held her face in her hands, and
kept holding it.

"Oh, you're all right," she said. "You're all right." She was crying, relieved and overjoyed. "You
brought my Nolly back. I'm so thankful. He's been missing for weeks."

That shook Már. She was grateful, and glad, and said so. She was sorry, she said; she had not known
he had been gone so long. When had he disappeared? How had it happened? Had he been here, and then
simply gone, and the others with him?

Etta would come round to those questions in time. For now she would not leave the reunion. She
wanted only to be with Már, and to rejoice that she was here, and that neither of them was dead,
though others were. When Már asked about Nolly, she turned the question aside, and came back each time
to the same thing: he was all right, and he was home, and Már had brought him.

Marigold was already awake, and heard it from a little way down the hall. She let them have their
space.

Soon after, food was brought to all of them. Nenuviel came round to see how everyone was. She was
limping, on a crutch with a makeshift extension, but looked well enough all things considered, and
she took particular care over Marigold, asking how the tending had gone for her, whether she felt any
better, whether she needed anything. Marigold's cheeks were blotched from crying through the night.
She gave no real answer. Nenuviel stepped closer and held out an arm, not presuming, asking. Marigold
let herself be hugged, and Nenuviel drew her in and kissed the top of her head and held her close.
Marigold felt her body loosen, a little.

Dwarves came to the rooms. They were welcome to rest, they said; or, if they chose, they might attend
the remembrance of the dead, which was about to begin. They explained what to expect and how it was
done, and told them they were invited but in no way bound.

Before they reached Marigold, a small Dwarf child burst in. It had plainly come in at the wrong door,
looking for somebody else. It stopped and stared at her very hard.

"Are you a child?" it asked. "Where's your hair?"

Marigold did not understand. Many Dwarf-women wore thick sideburns, and she had none.

"It runs in the family," she said.

The child nodded as if it understood entirely, and then went red, as though it had just remembered
that it had asked something rude, and fled to find the right room.

## The remembrance

Már and Etta were told that the remembrance would soon begin, and asked whether Már had eaten.
She had not, and they fed her before the two of them went. Marigold went, and Nenuviel.

Perry did not. In the ordinary way Perry was the most hospitable of them, always the one to go where
he was wanted. This time he shut himself in his room, wrapped in several Dwarf robes, ate as much as
he could, and was a long way from home.

It was not like any funeral the Company had known. There were two parts to it. The first was a
reciting of lineages: this was who they had been, and these were all those who had come before them.
The dead had been washed and laid out. Before each one were set precious things, things they had made,
or owned, or that bore on who they had been, and many people had brought small keepsakes as well. Some
of the dead wore beautiful clothes or jewels; some had their weapons or armour with them. That was the
second part: the things that would remain after they had decayed, by which the dead would always be
remembered. For some, people spoke of the halls and passages they had carved.

Nolly was there with Onar and Etta, standing alongside Már. He was attentive and aware, and he
did not shed a tear. He looked to be in shock. They walked past all the dead in turn.

Then that part ended, and they were led into another room, where there was food, and people talked
quietly. The dead were being taken to their resting places. Their kin would receive some of the
things that had been set before them, and some would be buried with them.

## The council

There Elin came to Már. There would be a formal sitting of the council, she said, to understand what
had happened, and to hear about Már and her guests. It would be after the midday meal and the rest
that followed it. Until then the hours were set apart, a sacred time. They might talk, or wander, or
rest; and then they were all bidden to the council.

Már knew something of the council, though she had never stood before it. There were seven, the
governing body of the Dwarves of the Blue Mountains. It was not quite like being brought before a
queen, and not quite like a town meeting either, but somewhat closer to the town. There was protocol
and procedure, and it was no small thing to be heard. With so little warning, she thought fewer than
seven would sit. And the council liked to be of one mind. It seldom was, and so those who felt
strongly, but not strongly enough to overthrow the whole matter, would stand aside and not vote. Four
in favour and three standing aside would carry it. Four in favour, two aside and one against would
not. It was a council much given to doing nothing.

The fine shield was hers now. Nobody had claimed it; it belonged to no family.

## The sacred hours

Perry came out of his room to find more food. In his robes, which were too small for him, he carried
the platter they had brought his breakfast on, and was piling it with Dwarf pastries and trying to
edge away when he was caught in a conversation about how important the council was going to be. He
did not listen. He ate. When the platter was empty he went looking for a bakery, meaning to take
whatever he could off whatever windowsill without talking to anybody.

It was Etta's bakery he robbed. Among her many specialities the greatest were her braided
beards: sweet plaited loaves, with jam and other fillings running through them in colours, like
ribbons woven through hair. There was one kind made with a nut that grew about those parts, roasted
and ground to a paste with pieces left whole, so that it was creamy and crunchy at once. Perry took a
selection. Within hours his room was a wreck, and he had built what nest he could out of the blankets
and pillows. He was not in the least prepared for the council.

As he ate, the weight of all he had seen grew less present. It did not go away. It became the kind of
hurt you do not feel until something presses on it. It showed itself as a clarity of purpose, and a
loss of innocence. Before, Perry had been the one who wanted everybody to have a good time, and to
feel that they belonged together. That was what he had been doing at the Havens. There had been bad
things in the world, of course: shakedowns by bandits on the road, and a shady man he knew who lied to
people and said things that were a little off. That had been as near to evil as he could imagine. Now
he had seen kin turn on kin and kill one another, and he knew it could have been him and his own
family; that it had only happened to be somewhere else. He came out of it among the crumbs of those
beautiful pastries, ready, and hardened, and having lost that part of himself.

Makheneb went to find a library. There were two near at hand, and he chose the larger. It was
meticulous records, for the most part. He had not known stone could have so many qualities. Here were
samples taken on such a day from such a place, what stone was in them, how much had been quarried, and
in what direction, which precious metals had been drawn out, which gems. There were so many words he
had never heard that he understood about one in four. He knew what a diamond was; the rest was beyond
him. What was not about stone was about the running of a people inside a mountain. He had found the
quartermasters' records, the stewards' accounts, the reckonings of several hundred years of Dwarves.

Marigold wandered and looked at everything, as one does in a strange town where everything is new and
nothing quite makes sense. It was a very different people from the one she had grown up among, but
there were things they shared, and she found them in the food, and in the way the Dwarves moved about
together. She had eaten at several tables now. At the Havens she had got into the kitchens before the
festival and traded recipes. Here too she was able to ask a few simple questions about how their
pastry was made, and she came away with a fair notion of how she might learn to make it herself,
when she was not grieving, and not getting ready for a council.

## What to say

Then the Company sat down together to decide what they would put before the council, and who would
speak for them.

Their errand had not changed. They had come to bring Nenuviel to look at the Cavity Stone and find out
what ailed it, on Thramli's behalf. That was still the first thing. Now they had much else to account
for as well, and there would be questions; and for all they knew, it was all one matter.

Should they speak of making a new tradition, when they did not yet know what was wrong? Someone
thought it might comfort the council to hear that the Company meant to give them something they could
act on, something to keep the thing in check, so long as they were honest that they did not know yet
what it would be. Nenuviel would be going back to the Havens once the task was done; she was there to
help with the lore. Perhaps the answer would be simply to seal the place, and to write into their
histories that it was sealed for good reason and must never, ever be unsealed.

What they needed was knowledge of how the thing worked on people. Who had been touched by it, and how
near had they been? Was there some threshold? Had they been in the room with it, or touched it, or
never come near it at all? How big was it? Could it be taken away, and dropped in the sea? Nobody knew.
Nolly, if it was the same thing that had taken him, had not been near it; the last he remembered was
simply being in the mountains. Could people be brought back? Nolly had been, and it had been kin that
did it. Perhaps it took those who loved them most, calling them home. They would need to watch Nolly,
someone said, for they had not yet seen him wholly recovered.

First, though, they had to convince the council that the thing was a threat, unless the council
already believed it. They could not afford to divide anyone. "It is dangerous; please, let us help
you help yourselves" was hard to say no to. The danger was that the Dwarves would not want help from
outside, and that was why it was good Már should lead: she was not from outside. So Már would speak
for them.

And there was the dead party's own errand. Thramli's company had gone to the Havens seeking this very
help. If the Company were only finishing what they had set out to do, then to refuse the Company was
to say those Dwarves had died for nothing. That was how they might honour the dead.

They might bring witnesses, like Nolly. Somebody suggested bringing one of those still afflicted
before the council, well bound, so that they could see for themselves. Már agreed, so long as he was
properly restrained.

But they did not yet know for certain that the Cavity Stone was the cause. There were a few hours
before the council. Each of them might do one thing in that time: go to see the ones who had been
bound, and how they were now; or find out from Nolly whether he was in any state to bear witness; or
learn who had sent Thramli in the first place, and what the story of that had been.

Then they would go before the council of the Dwarves of the Blue Mountains.
