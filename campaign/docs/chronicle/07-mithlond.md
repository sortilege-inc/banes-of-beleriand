## The Dwarven House

The Grey Havens lie on either side of the Firth of Lhûn, where it opens into the Gulf and the Gulf
opens into the sea. On the western shore is Haelond; on the eastern shore is Nevlond, where the
Company had come in. The Dwarven House stood in Nevlond, and despite its name it was not only for
Dwarves. It was where the Elves put anyone who was not an Elf and had no host.

Nevlond was greener than they had expected. From the balconies they could see gardens and trees
everywhere. Across the water Haelond looked grander, all fine stonework and craft, and in each city
there was a tower. The one in Nevlond served as a lighthouse, with a beacon at the top.

Those were days of rest. Lily's broken ribs began to knit, and by the end of their stay they had
healed. Makheneb and the others learned what they could of the things they had brought out of the
troll's den. Már's badger necklace was Dwarf-work from Erebor, with eyes and claws of green stones,
and there was power in it that made its wearer more formidable. The ruby ring was an Elvish ring
that brought with it a grace in courtesy. The golden circlet, with a pearl set in it, was also of
Elvish make, and it carried a blessing that could put heart into others. Makheneb, who thought
himself courteous enough already, gave the circlet to Perry, and Perry was delighted with it.

Perry had a new leather corslet, too, fitted close and made cunningly light, to replace the one he
had given up.

On their second day they began to notice the other guests. There were four Dwarves who kept a clockwork
routine. They had taken a corner of the common room for their own, where a couple of them played a
simple game with chips of stone, and they talked in low voices and smoked. Every so often one of them
would get up and speak to Edrain the steward, get a shake of the head, and sit down again. Már went
over to them. Their leader was a Dwarf named Thramli, and he was cordial. He had come to petition
Círdan and had not yet been granted an audience. He would say no more than that, and did not ask her
to sit down.

Lily noticed three of the Dúnedain together. She had never seen three at once. They had communities
somewhere, she knew, but in her experience they came through Bree singly or in pairs. There were two
women and a man, and they kept to themselves. One of them always seemed to hold a certain corner of the
bar while the other two went out with escorts, and they took it in turns, as though they were keeping
someone here to wait for something. Lily introduced herself and asked what brought them there. She got
a gentle rebuff, very polite, and a mention that they had been using the Arantarion, the library over
in Haelond.

## An audience

Perry asked his escort how one went about seeing Círdan. Did they just walk in? Would they be put on some
sort of list and made to wait all day?

His escort was hundreds of years old, and Perry had not been told the customs, and it showed in the
reply, which was the kind of answer one gives to a small child. "I'm afraid that audiences are somewhat
limited just now. If there were something of great importance, I could see it relayed. But I cannot say
how long the wait might be."

"Could you tell them," said Perry, "that Tom sent us?"

"I will make it known." The name meant nothing to her. It might mean something to someone else.

The Elves had given each of them an escort: Calalindir, a warden of the harbour; Ithrilas, a young scribe;
and Nenuviel, a poet of the Havens. Marigold asked hers where she might hear the stories the Elves told one
another. As it happened, there was to be a performance that very evening by Mellind, a singer and
storyteller of Haelond, and they all went.

## The Stone Garden

They crossed the firth to the sister city. The Stone Garden was a great plaza looking out over the gulf,
and its paving made patterns and pictures across the whole of it, like a canvas laid on the ground. Elves
stood and sat about it, eating and drinking and laughing. The performance ran from late afternoon until
dusk, through all the colours of the sunset, and the space carried sound beautifully. Red-leaved trees
flared in the last of the light.

Mellind came out and played a few notes on a flute. She had other instruments about her. Then she began to
tell a story. Makheneb could follow the words, though none of the names meant much to him; it seemed to
be a story from before the Elves came east to these shores, about lands and trees and countries across the
sea. The others understood none of it. They did not need to. The language was so musical that its feeling
came through the melody and the verse alone: beauty, and loss, and discovery, and a great strain and
struggle that ended in some great deed.

Perry cried through most of it. He had not understood a word.

Haelond had trees of its own, too: a tall, straight, grey kind, ninety feet high, strange to all of them,
whose resin scented the whole of that side of the city. It was used in the shipyards, and in the liquor.

## The pillars

On the way back up from the Stone Garden they passed two tall pillars, richly carved, each telling a story
in pictures. The left-hand one told of the Last Alliance of Elves and Men. The right-hand one commemorated
the siege of Barad-dûr, and the sacrifice of the Elves, and of the Dúnedain and their kings. None of it
meant much to the Company. It was like walking into a museum that had a very thorough history of some far
country for the last two and a half thousand years.

Lily asked Calalindir what he made of it. It was not a grey story. It did not pretend the enemy might have
been redeemed; but nor was it mere propaganda. It said plainly: here was the threat, here was what was
threatened, here was what had to be raised against it, here were the sacrifices, here was how it had been
won, and who had lived and who had died.

"I was present," said Calalindir, "at the defeat of the Witch-king, many years ago." That was about a
thousand years ago.

"Do you remember much of it? Does it feel like a thousand years?"

"Memory is a strange thing. There are years that stand out, and years that flow together. That was a tragic
and memorable year. I remember too much of it still." These pillars were a memorial to those who had not
come back: those for whom immortality had been denied. It was a strange way to put it, to the three who
would die of age. For the Elves, dying was not the way of things, except for those who died in times like
that, and it was a different kind of grief.

As they walked, they saw that many of the buildings were empty. They were not derelict, only tidy and
empty. They did not see any children.

They passed the bridge-gate, which was shut. Then they came to the tower on the Haelond side. It was called
the Tower of Lofty Light, and it had another name: the Arantarion, the name the Dúnedain had spoken of. The
library was there, and the forges were beneath it. Beside it stood a stone palace with roofs of red tile.
It had been the hall of Gil-galad, who founded the city, one of those who came long ago over the sea.
Galadriel, whose name only Makheneb knew, had once lived on the Nevlond side with others of her kin, and
had given it over to Círdan and gone away east; she dwelt now in Lothlórien. The Noldor were those Elves
who had come across the sea into Middle-earth, and the Sindar those who had never crossed it.

## The Arantarion

Lily and Marigold and Már went into the library.

It went down several levels underground, and everything in it was kept with a quiet rigour. It was room
upon room in a great ring, with paths between that felt almost like a maze; they came in at the centre and
found at least two rings of rooms and shelves around them, and at least three levels. None of them had ever
imagined so much writing in one place. There were scholars, and there were people making copies. Across the
way, one of the Dúnedain from the Dwarven House went by with a couple of scrolls, carrying them to a study
room.

Lily wanted to know how it was all arranged; surely it was not just put anywhere. An attendant explained.
A great deal of the library's wealth came from Elves on their way west over the sea. They took everything
of value with them when they left Middle-earth for ever, but a great many of their writings they left here.
Much of the archive was what remained of Elvish settlements all over the land, down the long years. Lily
thought of the empty buildings. The city must once have held three or four times as many as it did now.

Marigold asked for stories of monsters, and of anything like the banes. That, she was told, needed
authorisation. Instead, as a kindness, they offered her the histories written for children.

Lily slipped away and went, quietly, to where the Dúnedain were working. One of the women looked up. "Can I
help you?"

"Hi, yes. I just found this library. It's so huge; I'm just trying to get the lay of the land. What are you
all researching?"

"Who are you?" It was not a question about her name. It was a question about what right she had to ask.

Lily told her she was a fellow traveller with a project of her own, that they had met some dark things on
their travels, and that they were trying to learn where such things came from. She told her about the Orc
in the Moors.

The woman sat up at that. Lily was not, then, entirely a stranger to the wild. Her name was Laeroneth. "We,
too, have met things that are not in their proper places," she said. "We hope to learn more here. It is a
wonderful resource. You should make use of it."

Lily asked whether she could borrow things. Unfortunately, Laeroneth said, they did not lend. You could study
here for years unless you knew how to find what you wanted. But the attendants were very helpful, if you could
tell them what you were looking for and they believed you had good reason. "If you find yourself getting
nowhere, come to me, and I'll see if I can help."

Lily asked one of the librarians whether a Ranger named Findemir had ever brought anything here, or read
anything. The head of the library was Galdor, though it was not Galdor she spoke to. They were quite sure that
Findemir had never visited.

## The forges

Calalindir took Makheneb and Perry to the forges.

There were forges on several scales. There were smithies for weapons and armour, and those, it seemed, were for
beginners. Then there were the great forges where they made things for ships and buildings, working in tons.
The whole tower had been built around them; the heat of the forges was drawn off and led through it, and the
air brought in from outside was warmed and sent all over the tower. They were sweating a little.

Calalindir brought them to Gaethel of the Anvil, who had charge of all of it. "One of our guests, Makheneb of
Mirkwood, has a question for you."

Makheneb laid the great bow on the bench, and the broken Númenórean sword from the ruins. "I wonder whether you
know anything of the history of these. There seems to be some kind of curse on the bow, at least. I wonder
whether you know anything of the nature of that sort of thing."

"A curse?" said Perry. Nobody had told him there was a curse on the bow. The only curses he had heard of were
the terrible kind, where everyone around was infected. "How do we fix this?"

Gaethel picked up the bow and turned it over and looked at it, and handed it back. "There is indeed a darkness in
this. If you will allow us, we could keep it here, and try to understand what we can of the nature of the curse.
I cannot promise that we can remove it." He would send word to the Dwarven House.

Then he turned to the sword. "This is of Númenórean make. It is a good sword. We could reforge it."

Makheneb had no skill with swords. It should be Perry's, he said. Perry asked whether it could be made into
something more like an axe. Gaethel said that was very tractable indeed.

"We will get back to you about the bow," he said. "We will make sure you get it back. I do not like keeping cursed
things about indefinitely."

"Neither do I," said Makheneb.

## Círdan will see you

When they came back into the common room of the Dwarven House, Edrain came to them. "Círdan will see you in the
morning."

Thramli was at Már's side at once. "How did you manage an audience?"

"You know Tom?" said Már. It had all come down, she explained, to some honey in Perry's pocket. Apparently that
mattered to Círdan. "That's pretty much it. I wish I understood more, to be honest."

"Would you bring our petition before Círdan on our behalf?"

Már had meant to offer to help them anyway. "What is the petition? If I can help, I'd like to."

"I'll buy some drinks," said Thramli. "Come and sit down. There are a few pieces to understand." And Már sat at
the Dwarves' table.

High in the Blue Mountains, Thramli said, there was a thing called the Cavity Stone. It was very old, and there
was a group of Dwarves whose work was to keep the place where it lay. It was a place that was left as it was and
tended, the way a Dwarf-hall keeps a part of itself as it has always been and goes and builds new things elsewhere;
it mattered for its stonework, and it mattered more deeply than that. The stone itself had not been made by
Dwarves. It had been found. And it was doing strange things to the minds of those who kept it. They were starting
to crack. It had taken the Dwarves thirty years to work out that the stone was the thing all the troubles had in
common. When they cut off someone's exposure to it at the first signs, the effects stopped growing worse, but they
did not go away, and nobody had found a way to undo them.

Mostly it showed itself as disturbances of the mind. One of the keepers had died in an accident. It might have been
carelessness, the kind of carelessness that comes over someone who knows every safety rule inside out and one day
simply does not follow them. Another had threatened violence, and had to be restrained and kept apart, watched,
and allowed no weapons. And some of them had begun to speak in strange tongues. The Dwarves had tried to write down
what they said, as it sounded, and did not know what language it was.

Did it affect animals? There were no animals kept near it, so they did not know.

Thramli gave Már the scraps of paper. They were copies of the ones meant for Círdan.

Had they looked in the library? They had not. They had been told they were not permitted.

"Why?" Már asked. They did not know. They had only been told.

That struck Már as suspicious. Lily had been let into the library without anyone asking her anything. And the steward
who kept the Dwarves from Círdan was the same one to whom all the escorts answered.

Meanwhile one of the Dúnedain, the man, came over to Lily. "Círdan hasn't been seeing anyone," he said. "How did you
manage it? I thought the returning ship would take all his attention for some time."

"The returning ship?"

"You didn't hear? There was a ship of Elves heading west. It was turned back by strange storms, and it has had to
come home. It has taken up most of their attention. The ones at the top are trying to work out whether it is only for
now, and what it means. I've never heard of a voyage being turned back like that."

Lily told him that Laeroneth had kindly offered to help them in the library, and asked whether he would like her to
mention their business to Círdan. He thanked her. "I don't feel our questions are well put yet. In time, with enough
research, I hope we can bring Círdan a question worthy of him."

## Canthwë

Marigold is a hobbit, and she loves her pipe as much as any hobbit. As soon as she came into the Dwarven House that
evening she could smell pipe-weed, and she could have told you exactly which leaf it was, and within a few years which
harvest. In the corner sat an old man, a Man by the look of him and not an Elf, with a long beard, smoking. She had not
seen him before. He caught her staring, and held up his pipe. "Smoke with me."

His name, he said, was Canthwë. He liked walking about the city best of all. He loved the green things, and did a
little gardening, trimming this and that. He was not an Elf, but he seemed to go wherever he liked, and to see Círdan
whenever he pleased; he was some kind of friend of the household, or servant, or a member of it in some loose way that
was never made clear. He lived in the city and came to the Dwarven House to sit sometimes.

His talk came and went. He would ask Marigold about herself, and then he would go off on strange tangents, long and
winding, about little things: how certain beasts of the field were not what you thought, and the animal you took for a
horse was nothing of the kind and no relation to any horse. Then he would come back round to some fellow he had met down
by the docks, and what they had done. Marigold grew very comfortable.

It was not a strong leaf; pipe-weed only relaxes you, and is certainly not meant to show you visions. At first she felt
loose and easy all over. Then everything began to turn monstrous, and the monstrousness was sucked quickly away, and
something glorious took its place; and it went on happening, over and over, as though terrors kept coming out of a
cupboard and somebody kept slamming the door on them and unrolling a beautiful tapestry in front of it. She lost track of
what he was saying. The visions were so vivid that she found herself looking round at them, and almost speaking to them,
as if they were really there.

When it settled at last, Canthwë was gone. Most of the house had gone to bed. Marigold sat there with her body loose and
her heart racing, and with the aftertaste of having seen something glorious, and something else that disturbed her and
that she could not quite remember.

## Before Círdan

In the morning they made themselves ready. They had no time to go to the market and buy better clothes; they would have to
do with what they had, made as clean as possible. Perry had washed his hair and slicked it back, and was wearing what he
thought of as his best. It was not presentable. He had the Elvish circlet on, and his trouser-legs were tied on, all three
times over. It was certainly a look.

Már did her best to resolve not to be hot-headed. Other people were counting on her for their health. She meant to watch
Círdan closely and find the right time to raise the Dwarves' petition.

They talked over what they wanted. Lily wanted to get into the library properly. They all wanted help with what they had
seen, with what they were going to see, and with what to do about it. Why was the white beast *the weapon against* anything?
Why was the fanged woman so frightening, and bald? Did he know anything of the long-folk? Was there some original charge, some
first set of instructions, that the Númenóreans had been given and that had come down to the Rangers like a message passed
along a line, getting fainter at every step? Círdan was so old that he might simply know. Perry had given Círdan's honey
exactly the one nibble. He had also dipped into it a few times since, because his pocket knife was in the same pocket.

Edrain himself took them to the tower of Círdan. It rose from an eight-sided base, and the tiles were worked with patterns
of the sea, and there were shells set into the stone. Inside it was clean and bright, and the windows were set so that the
sun came in without any glare, and the whole place was warm, as though they were outside though they were indoors. They went
up to a mezzanine, and up again, to a room where guests were received, with tables and small refreshments laid out. Then the
doors opened.

They had seen a great many Elves by now, and almost none of them had beards. Círdan had a beard, and grey hair. For Makheneb
he was the oldest Elf he had ever seen, by a long way. He guessed at five thousand years, and more.

Círdan came forward. "A friend of Iarwain Ben-adar!" he said, and embraced them. "Welcome, welcome, welcome, friends!"

"Círdan, right?" said Perry. "I'm guessing you know Tom?"

"Iarwain Ben-adar, as we call him. Yes. He is older even than I."

"And you don't look a day over sixty."

"It has been an age, and an age, and an age," said Círdan.

"Well," said Perry, "we're on a sort of quest for him. We're helping the Rangers of the North with a spot of bother they're
having, with these giant creatures that sometimes are and sometimes aren't asleep, all over the place. Oh, and also: honey.
Here you go."

For a moment Círdan simply held it. Then he fetched a bowl and put the honey in it, and smelled it, and dipped his fingers in
and tasted. "It is still good," he said. "This is a perfect gift."

Perry apologised for having had a bit. Círdan was not in the least concerned.

## The council

Már began by singing *Ghostcleavers*, to show him what they had faced. Círdan listened politely, and just as she was about to
go back into the chorus he cut her off. "I apologise for the rudeness. I am rather pressed for time today, because of other
duties. But if you have particular questions or concerns, I can address them."

Lily gave him the short version, with no song. They were working with Tom and the Rangers, looking into these banes, and they
had a great many questions, and they had come to him because of his wisdom, and his knowledge, and the library. She would like
to be allowed into the library properly, please.

Halfway through, Marigold caught a smell she knew from the night before. Canthwë had wandered in and was eating the food off
the side tables.

When Lily had finished, Círdan nodded. "Ah, yes. I am familiar with some of these. You call them banes. That is a useful name."
He pointed at Canthwë. "One such thing, more a place than a thing, corrupted one of the Istari. I have taken care of it."

Már and Makheneb knew the word. The Istari were the Wizards; they knew of at least a couple of them. Canthwë, it seemed, was
one, and one of these banes had corrupted him, and Círdan had that in hand. To Perry and Lily and Marigold it sounded rather
as though Círdan's gardener had got corrupted, and Círdan had seen to it. Perry heard *Istari*, and supposed Canthwë had once
been a star. It had not been here, Círdan said, but far to the east, beyond the Misty Mountains, further east even than
Mirkwood. "I am making sure that he is no danger to himself or others. He is capable of great power, and he could be a great
danger."

Then Már gave him the scraps of paper from the Dwarves. It took him a moment to make sense of them, because they were written
as they sounded. Then his face hardened.

"Who said this?"

"The Dwarves who kept the stone," said Már, "before they were taken from their work. They were the ones."

"This is the Black Speech," said Círdan. "This is the language of the Enemy." He paused. "I will hear the rest of you out. We
will come back to this."

Perry asked his question. "Why haven't we been corrupted? And what should people do so that they aren't?"

Círdan looked at him with compassion. "Much of what I have heard of your folk seems to be greatly out of date," he said. "You
have a tremendous courage about you. You say this as though you mean to seek out the rest of them."

"In fact, that's our quest," said Perry. He took out his grandfather's map and spread it on the floor. "We've been to two. This
was the wolf, the giant white beast. This was the fanged woman. Both asleep. They both seemed all right. And we're heading here,
and here." He pointed to the Blue Mountains, and south of Tharbad. "But we're pretty lost. We only know we're supposed to be
checking on them. When we get to the Blue Mountains we won't know what we're looking for, or how to get in. Maybe it's with the
Dwarves; maybe it's this stone. What we want to know is where it is, and what we're looking for, and how to deal with it."

"You have been working with Tom," said Círdan. "Tom is many things. One thing he is not is organised." He looked round at them.
"Do you urgently need to be anywhere?"

"Is there an urgency about these banes?" asked Marigold. "Should we go and see to them as soon as we can? And what does seeing to
them look like?"

"There are urgencies of different kinds," said Círdan. "I am dealing with a crisis now that must be resolved by Mereth Bantiel, a
festival at the end of summer, a couple of months from now. After that I can sit down with you and we can make a plan together:
what you would need, and whom I could send with you, to the Blue Mountains or wherever we decide. I would welcome your company
until then. You would have the run of the cities. We will find you quarters of your own; you need not stay at the Dwarven House.
You will be my guests. This is a matter of great weight and delicacy, and I do not wish you to run headlong into anything that
would put you in danger. I do not believe that a matter of months will make a great difference to this."

Lily asked whether they could help with the ship.

Every year at the end of summer, at Mereth Bantiel, Círdan said, they sent a ship into the West, with any Elves who were leaving
these lands. The ship now returning was last year's. It had not yet arrived, but it had sent a message ahead of it. In the next few
days they would send ships out to meet it, because it sounded as though those aboard had suffered greatly, and the ship was hardly
seaworthy; it had not sunk, but it might not make it home. And there was this year's ship, with its passengers already listed.
Should they send both out again? Wait a year? What should become of the passengers? Those were the questions before him. Within a
few weeks some of his people would go out to understand things better, and then there would be decisions.

Then he turned back to Már. What was her concern?

"How are the Dwarves supposed to protect themselves?" she asked. "They have people affected whom they can't treat. How are they
supposed to fight something they can't strike?"

"What is your own part in it? How well do you know this stone, and the people who keep it?"

Only what they had told her, said Már. It had been brought to her attention by other Dwarves; she had never seen it herself. But it
sounded like the things the Company had been seeing near the banes: the dead, and the man frozen, perhaps cursed.

"Some of the Enemy's devices have the power to warp the minds of the weak," said Círdan. "I do not mean that unkindly. In the face of
great power, many of us are weak. The speech you have shown me is strengthening the effect. As a first measure, those who speak such
things must be kept apart from the thing itself. I do not think the danger is a matter of months. But it is like water set over a
fire: you may heat it for a long time before it boils, and once it boils it boils. The water has been heated, and it is not yet
boiling, and these words are more heat. If that speech goes on in the presence of that thing, it will come to the boil the sooner."

"So it's only the speaking near the stone? If they speak like that miles away, it isn't as much of a problem?"

"It should matter very little, by comparison."

He would send someone back with the Dwarves, he said, to see that the right measures were kept to in the meantime. It would not be
an answer, but it would keep things from growing worse than they need while a better answer was found.

"Thank you," said Már. She felt easier knowing they would have someone who knew.

Then they were dismissed, and it was late morning.

## What they made of it

On the way back they talked it over. Már was surprised that there was anything more important than the banes. They had brought him
this, and he had said, in effect, *we will get to it when we get to it.* Lily thought it made more sense than it seemed. Nothing
like the ship had ever happened before; the Dúnedain said so, and the Elves, she thought, did not get rattled very often. Perhaps
it was a perfect storm. Perhaps even the storm was connected to another bane. There were fifty things on the list now, and she
did not like any of them.

At least he was sending someone to the Dwarves. And Círdan, they agreed, had plainly been charmed by Perry. They had better keep that
friendship strong.

Lily had looked over, while Círdan was talking about the minds of the weak, and seen Canthwë eating the vegetables off a tray with his
fingers, taking a bite and dipping back in.

## The villa

When they got back to the Dwarven House, Edrain was waiting, with their escorts and a few others. Their new quarters were ready. They
could gather their things and go, and their horses and ponies would be brought over.

It was a villa in a very fine part of Nevlond. There were abandoned buildings here too, though fewer than on the Haelond side. It
stood three storeys high, and it had been partly refurnished for them, some things taken out and new things brought in and the whole
place freshened up. Each of them had several rooms to themselves, with common spaces to share. And each was given a little clasp to
wear, which showed that they had Círdan's favour. They could go freely through both cities, and use the library, and their requests
would be taken rather more seriously from now on.

Lily was not used to living in a house where everyone had their own rooms, and she went round them all in the mornings, waking
everybody up. The only one who was ever up before her was Marigold.

## The weeks of summer

Summer went on in Mithlond, and the Company settled into its days.

Már went to the markets. The weavers made a cloth that was very light and breathed beautifully, and the Elves wore it in overlapping
layers, so that its colours showed through one another and blended. The outer layers often carried designs a little out of line with
each other, and as the wearer moved the designs shifted against one another. The glassmakers laid glass on glass in different colours,
in intricate patterns, and made beautiful jars. And there was a wine found nowhere else, made with the resin of the tall grey trees of
Haelond, and with seaweed in it too, strong-tasting and heady.

On the Nevlond side the gardens were a riot of every kind of plant, and Marigold drew them in her book.

Már went back to see the Dwarves. Thramli thanked her over and over. They were going home, and with them someone whom Círdan trusted
as a right hand, who would hold things together until Círdan could send more, probably some time in the autumn. The Dwarves owed her a
great favour. If she ever came up their way, into the Blue Mountains towards the Halls of the Dwarves, they would gladly host her. And
if she happened to come across anything in the library about a certain very old disagreement over who had been first to a certain
thing, anything at all that supported his side of it, Thramli would be much obliged. It was the kind of petty feud that is so very

Lily spent half of every day in the library, with or without anything particular to look for, and sat with the Dúnedain when they would
let her. She told them a little more of what her company was about, and of Findemir, and Moriel. And they told her theirs. They had found
some old Númenórean things they could not identify, and the place where they had found them, and they were trying to learn more about
both. Lily's history with Findemir, and the fact that Marigold had taken up Findemir's errand when he fell, made an impression on them.

When she was not at her books she was in the stables. She took care of Buddy, and she followed the Elves who worked with the animals and
asked them a thousand questions and tried to learn what she could. The Elves had not perfected the breeding of horses, but they had come
a long way; theirs were some of the most magnificent beasts she had ever seen. Their chief experience of mortality, living here, was their
animals, which came and went in the blink of an eye. They shared their knowledge freely. Buddy had charms of his own, and no one was rude
to him.

Perry went to the gatehouses. It was where he felt most at home. At first they rebuffed him: *we are trying to do a job here; go and see
the city.* But he must have mentioned it to Edrain in passing, because a place was made for him, a small and carefully limited one, where
he could help and could watch. There were not the crowds there had been at the gates of Bree. What was remarkable was who came. There were
Elves from Mirkwood, and one from Lothlórien. Some brought whole wagons, and some came alone on a single horse. All of them came with the
same soberness. They had lived all their lives in the east, and they were never going back, and they were coming here to wait for a ship
that might or might not sail at the end of summer, into a future none of them knew. Perry never had the chance to sit down with any of them,
but their manner was muted, and the sadness of it weighed on him for a long while.

Perry was also afraid of the sea, and he set out to cure himself of it. At first he only watched the water. Low tide in Haelond left it only
three feet deep, and that he could manage; he started by putting his toes in. Out on the flats at low tide there was an older Elf, older
than Makheneb though nothing like as old as Círdan, who caught crabs and other things with his hands. His name was Otto. He had been a
sailor once, and he was not quite ready to go west, and he much enjoyed waiting, and cooking. He became Perry's friend. Perry learned about
the sea from him, and listened to his stories, and went to his crab boils, and one day walked all the way in, chest deep, and looked round
very proudly, as if he did this all the time.

Perry had also never had so many taverns to go to, and the cities were far more crowded than Bree, however empty the buildings. He made
friends in them, and drank the local wine, and lived, within reason, on Círdan's account.

Makheneb saw about new armour, and practised, and every now and then disappeared for days at a time into the forests of Lindon to hunt.
Coming back from one of these, he found a message from Gaethel waiting for him: he should come and collect his bow. The smiths had cleansed
it. The Shadow that had been upon it no longer weighed on the one who carried it. Something of it still lingered, Gaethel warned. It would
make it harder for him to move unseen among his enemies; it would draw their attention when he was near. Orcs, at the very least.

He went and found Perry to tell him. He had it back, and it was even better than before.

## The ship

Meanwhile Círdan's ships were readying to go out to meet the returning one. Már and Lily were interested in going. Marigold would take any
chance to see something new. Perry would not go on a boat. Makheneb thought it might be better if he stayed; he would be too tempted to keep
going west.

In the end, they stayed in the Havens, and waited to see what the ship would bring.
