## Before the festival

Summer was drawing to its end, and the Havens were making ready for Mereth Bantiel. A small ship was
going out to meet the one that was coming home, and the Company could have gone with it. Nobody had
asked them to; they would have had to volunteer. The festival was the reward, somebody said, and the
voyage would be the punishment, and why would anyone choose the punishment? None of
them went. They stayed in the villa and waited. Their own departure would come after the festival;
there would still be time to reach the Blue Mountains before winter.

As the preparations quickened, something else crept into them. Everyone knew about the returning
ship by now, and it had not yet come. So the Havens made ready for a feast with one eye always on the
sea, as though bad news might come over the horizon at any moment. It was like getting ready for the
midwinter holiday while a grandmother lies ill, and nobody yet knows how ill.

Lily felt it most. Her answer, when things grew tense, was to keep her hands busy. She had gone to the
library to study and found herself putting books away, and after that she was there every morning,
shelving, whether anyone had asked her to or not. At the stables she did the same, cleaning and
counting and checking whatever needed doing.

There she noticed Buddy's friend. The Elves' horses were not great warhorses, but they were taller
than Buddy, and lean and rangy, strong without bulk. One of them was a white horse called Levlay.
When they let the horses out to run in the great field, Buddy could never keep up, and Levlay would
circle back and run beside him. The two of them were friends. The stablemaster said to Lily that
Buddy had settled in very well, and was flourishing among the Elves' horses. Then he told her about
the horse-run. At one point in the festival the horses would run through the streets, through one city
and over the bridge into the other, a procession that was more of a stampede. The roads had to be
made ready for it. A couple of lead horses went first and the rest learned to follow. Buddy had simply
been left out of it, of course. But Levlay was so keen on him that the stablemaster wondered whether
Lily would be willing for Buddy to run too.

Lily said it would be an honour. She said it as coolly as she could. As soon as the stablemaster's back
was turned, she did a little dance.

## Mablin

Perry spent his spare hours on the walls, looking out to sea. He had learned about the Straight Road, the
way across the sea to the West, and that the westbound ship had been turned back somewhere along it and
had sent word by bird. His clasp from Círdan let him go most places, with an escort, and it was not a time
of emergency, so they let him up into the towers that looked out over the water.

He was not the world's greatest thinker, but he was in tune with what people were feeling. He knew by now,
from Otto, that this Third Age was an age of loss, and that the Elves were passing on to something better,
and he felt the weight of that. He wanted to talk with people about better days, and to hear about times
that had been good. He found a guard named Mablin, who had the air of a grandfather and a grandfather's
knack for stories: not the kind where you sit down and are told a story, but the kind where something
comes up and he says, *oh, that reminds me*, and gives you five minutes of a whole world you never knew
existed. He would not be made to teach anything in order. Perry tried, and he resisted. The only way to
learn from him was in scraps. But Perry soaked those up with such eagerness that Mablin warmed to him.

Perry came to understand two things about him. Mablin wanted very much to go west. And he did not want to
leave. He did not want to give all this up. He wanted to go, and he wanted to stay, and now there was the
news, and the fear that the way might be shut and the choice taken out of his hands. That whole season
was bittersweet to him, and he kept it at bay by telling Perry stories of the ages before.

## You weren't there

Makheneb had very little patience for any of it. The Elves were in a state because one ship had not come
home. Makheneb had come from the east, and in the east things were already bad. The Elves of the Havens,
it seemed to him, were sitting in a comfortable hidey-hole without the faintest idea.

One evening there was a gathering outdoors, low-key, with food and drink, people coming and going.
Makheneb had been keeping to himself. Nearby a couple of Elves were talking, and they mentioned the Battle
of Five Armies in passing, as a little blip out in the east, and dismissed it.

Makheneb had known Elves who died in that battle. He began to recite their names. He knew them all, and he
said them one after another, and he grew louder and angrier as he went. "You weren't there," he told them.

It killed the evening. People drifted away awkwardly, and went home. But to their credit, word got round.
Now and then after that some Elf would come up to him out of nowhere and say, *we are so sorry for your
losses.* And the rest gave him room.

## Nutmeg bread

Marigold did not understand why it mattered so much that the ship should come back, or why people went
anywhere at all, and every time somebody explained it to her she lost the thread. She went looking for
whoever still had some joy in them, and found her way into the kitchens.

They were mostly in Nevlond: great kitchens for feeding crowds, in the middle of the long preparations
before a feast, when every sauce and every spice-mix is made ahead so that on the day it can all be put
together. The Elves of Lindon used almost all the same things that grew in the Shire, apart from what came
from the sea, but they put them together in their own proportions, and the result was quite different.
Marigold rolled up her sleeves. She traded recipes. And she took one of their breads and changed it a
little, the way changing the proportions a little in baking gives you something new, and made it hers: a
braided loaf, flaky, with nutmeg in it, that you could tear chunks off in the street. She made it so many
times, at such a scale, that she had it perfect.

The kitchens were full of talk about the festival: which songs, which singers, something with the boats in
the harbour, perhaps even fireworks. Marigold collected all of it and made herself the Company's guide.
This was happening on this street, and then a couple of hours later they would have to be over here for
this. There was a storyteller she was especially excited about. Nobody else was as excited as she was.

At the villa the Company mostly passed one another in the halls, coming and going. Lily called a family
meal every day, and insisted on it. Perry slept when he could, as he always had, since his old master at the
gate had given him such wild shifts; and every night he went to bed an hour later than the night before,
until he had lapped everyone else and was falling asleep in his porridge. Once, at one of the family
dinners, he woke up long enough to say *take me to that* about somebody's day, and fell asleep again. The
villa was a place to leave your things and to sleep, and to see each other now and then. Otherwise there
was far too much to see.

## Mereth Bantiel

The festival began with a line of ships going out through the harbour. Then the horses were brought down to
the water's edge, and they came charging up through the streets of Nevlond, over the bridge into Haelond, all
the way up to the gates and round again, back towards the stables, and Buddy ran with them beside Levlay.
They had seen the ships at the quays and on the water, but they had never seen them under full sail,
sweeping across the horizon.

After that the festival did not stop for three or four days. There were Elves who were not great ones for
parties, but everyone was, for this, as people are on the last night of the year. Because the city was not as
full as it might have been, all the life of it poured into a few streets and a few quarters, which blazed
while the rest of the city lay dark and quiet. It went on into the small hours and eased into the next day
without stopping: some went home to sleep, and others were up at dawn gathering crabs on the beach for the
morning. Yssiongorn, the wine of the Havens, flowed very freely.

Perry had been hibernating for this. He was awake for all of it. He took naps in the corners of taverns and
led crawls from one to the next, and grew rowdier every night. Makheneb, who had always preferred the night
and the dark, was not a reveller. He was the sort who, pulled into a crowd, slips quietly out of it. Perry
kept bringing him drinks, and Makheneb kept pouring them out.

Marigold's plan lasted until the middle of the second day, when it became clear they could not coordinate
anything. She had bitten off more than she could chew. She took naps behind rocks and then went on to the next
thing. The Elves' food came in very small portions and an enormous variety, far too many dishes ever to try
them all, and she tried to try them all, like someone at a fair who has resolved to visit every stall. Lily
darted about trying to see everything.

## The ship

Before dawn on the third day, Lily was still up. She had stayed up all night, determined to see everything,
and now she was helping clean up and still drinking a little, since if you help clear up you get free wine.
The light was just beginning in the east.

Then she heard it. It was faint at first, a wailing. It went on, and it grew, and it began to come from more
than one direction. People came out of their doors, curious, and began to walk towards the shore, and as they
walked, they too broke out wailing. Everyone was heading for the water.

The Company found one another near the shore. The horizon was golden and pink, and the light lay scattered on
the water. A ship was coming in. Its mast was broken, and a makeshift rig had been raised on half a mast. There
was only one ship. There should have been two: the returning ship, and the one that had gone out to meet it.
Five or eight small boats were rowing out to it, and a larger ship was waiting further off, holding back because
the broken ship was coming in so slowly without its mast.

Along the shore Elves were on their knees weeping, or holding one another, or talking in confused and frightened
voices.

Lily looked at the ship, and then looked again, and understood. It was not the ship that had gone out to meet
the returning one. It was the westbound ship itself, the *Lómelindë*. Of the other ship, the *Gwaeloth*, there was
no sign at all.

## After

The *Lómelindë* came into harbour, and those aboard came ashore gaunt and broken. Only a third of them had come
back.

The festival was over. Lily and Marigold made the braided nutmeg bread, a great deal of it, and carried it to the
houses where the Elves off the ship had been taken in. There they learned the stark truth. The *Lómelindë* had
never met the *Gwaeloth* at all. And of those who had sailed in her, two in every three were gone.

Perry went to find Mablin, and found him at the watchtower, staring out with hollow eyes, smoking. The death of an
Elf, Mablin told him, was like the death of a human child. So much future was stolen, even from one who had lived
for centuries. After that he said nothing, and Perry sat beside him and smoked with him in silence.

Makheneb had already packed his things.

That evening Edrain came to them. Círdan would see them in the morning.

## Canthwë

Outside the Arantarion in the morning they met Canthwë again. He was puffing contentedly at his pipe and blowing
little circles and triangles and squares of smoke.

Marigold said good morning and asked how he was.

He was doing wonderfully, he said.

"Aren't you sad?"

"Sad? About what?"

"About the ships," said Marigold. "The Elves that didn't come back."

He seemed to remember. "Oh, yes. That's awful. That's terrible." They pressed him, and all at once, with startling
clearness, he began to name them: every Elf who had sailed, on both ships, and whether each had come back or not.
For the *Lómelindë*, one in every three had come back. For the *Gwaeloth*, none.

A moment later the clearness had gone, as suddenly as it came. He was smoking happily again, as though he had never
said a word of it.

The Company understood then what had been done to him. Whatever Círdan had done to Canthwë's mind worked like a lid
that shut itself again the moment anything rose beneath it.

## Círdan's charge

They climbed the tower and came before Círdan.

He began with Makheneb. He apologised for the careless jesting about battles long past, and for the weight of memory
and loss it had laid on him. He spoke a little of the death of Elves, who by nature do not die. Then he turned to the
others and asked each of them how long they thought they had left to live.

Perry guessed forty years. Lily said fifty. Marigold said she did not know.

Against lives so short, he laid his charge on them.

They had seen the banes and touched them, and they understood something of them. The banes ought, in the end, to be
destroyed. The Company did not have the power to destroy them. But it could make sure that they were safe: safe for
this generation and for every generation after it. That was their task. They must go to each place and make sure
there was a way of doing things that would last across the generations. It did not matter what it was, a law, or a
rite, or a religion, or a story, so long as every generation knew not to touch the hot stove. What mattered was that
it was never broken.

Nenuviel, his aide, was going north to the Cavity Stone in the Blue Mountains, with the Dwarves. The Company would go
with her. And he would go on sending ships into the West, twice a year, until the Straight Road opened again, or until
Lindon had spent itself.

They asked more. Tom's list, it turned out, was too small. There were not only four of these things. Four were on Tom's
map; there was the place in the east that had taken Canthwë; and there was at least one other that nobody knew anything
about at all.

Canthwë, Círdan told them, had soaked up the curse of that place in the east the way a rag soaks up wine. That frightened
them. They were being sent round to clean up places like that. Were they at risk of becoming like him? Would he have to
put them down?

Círdan was taken aback. Then he realised he had been speaking too simply, and needed to be simpler still. "Have you met
Gandalf?" he asked.

"The fireworks man?"

So he told them. He told them of the Valar, and of the Maiar, great and powerful beings, and how a few of them had come
into Middle-earth to help in different ways, and some had taken on the likeness of Men. Gandalf was one of them, powerful
beyond anything they knew and hiding it very modestly. Such beings could command the weather, and call fire, and move
stone, and open what was shut, each in their own way. They were called the Istari, the Wizards. Canthwë had once gone by
another name, which Círdan had sealed. He had been very powerful, and he had been working a great power in that place,
and the curse had drawn on that power. That was the tragedy of it: the greatness of his power was what made him take the
curse into himself. "But this is not a working you are at any risk of performing," said Círdan, "and I do not believe the
other banes you meet pose that same danger. You do not stand where he stood, and you cannot do what brought it on him."

Perry did not believe a word of it. There were things he knew about the world, and fireworks-makers turning out to be
wizards was not one of them, and Canthwë was plainly just a man who smoked too much. Lily thought some version of it might
be true. Marigold listened gravely.

Then Círdan spoke of his own fear. He feared that the Beast of Belegaer had woken in the sea. He gave them a scrap of script
with its name written in the Black Speech. Long ago, he said, a Silmaril had been lost in the deep, and he feared it might
have roused the creature. He did not know what kind of tradition could ever settle a thing that lay in the sea. But if the
jewel could be taken from it, perhaps the beast could be settled again.

Lily asked him not to send another ship yet. "Wait until spring," she said. They would go north into the mountains now, and
come back down in the spring, and go with it then. Círdan agreed.

Before they left, Lily found the chance to ask Laeroneth, and another of the Dúnedain, Meneldil, about Makheneb's great bow,
the Great Bow of Angnir. They suggested that she look for the tellers of old tales in certain towns, who carried such stories
down.

## Keeping the fire

They talked it over among themselves afterwards.

The long-folk, at least, had a tradition already; they kept the fanged woman asleep, and they were not well known, so nobody
was seeking them out. The first bane had a tradition too, and a well-kept one, though the Company would tell the Rangers it
needed doing a good deal more often than every forty years. The bigger question there was who had been trying to break into it.
One of those men had come through Bree, and they had come from the south.

Círdan had told them what he could. He did not himself know what these things were, apart from what he had told them of Canthwë
and of the Beast of Belegaer; what he knew of the Blue Mountains was what they had just told him. What he advised was this: that
the peoples bound to each place, and to its tradition, should find allies and stay in touch with one another, so that they
could gather together if any one of them faltered, or if something broke loose. He suspected that the answer at each place
would be different. Anything the Company could work out, and set down as a plan, even one they could not carry out themselves,
would help. What they wanted in the end was something that would still be there generations from now, so that if an emergency
came there would be a known way to call for help.

Perry had an idea. They could cut great stacks of wood and set them up between all the places, and if a place needed help, it
would light its fire. There would have to be strict punishments for lighting one for no reason, and riders with buckets of
water for when it was an accident. The thought of learning each place's ways well enough to build a tradition that made sense
there, and that would work within it, excited Perry more than anything else had.

Somebody asked whether the Cavity Stone was the bane of the Blue Mountains. Círdan had been careful not to say, but he had
seemed to think so. They would not have to go to the Dwarves, solve one problem, and then go fifty miles on and solve another.
Probably.

## The ride north

Círdan knew he was asking a great deal of them, and he equipped them well. Each of them could have their pick of a horse fit for
the mountains. Leaper was retired, and left in good hands. Perry took Levlay, Buddy's friend. Lily kept Buddy, who was sleek and
well fed now and no longer anything like half-starved. Strawberry went home: one day Tom simply turned up at the stables,
petting her, and said he was taking Strawberry back to the Old Forest. They were given better gear, too, Elvish work that did the
same job at a third of the weight. And Makheneb had his bow back, with its Shadow lifted from it.

Perry laid out their route, straight and direct, since there was no point adding the weight of the mountains to their journey
before they had to. It was the lovely end of summer, and the weather was glorious. They rode north and made excellent time.

Ahead lay the valley of the Dwarves. But as the valley opened before them, a shadow of despair began to gather.
